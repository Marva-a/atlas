out: Tell apart policies, standards, procedures and guidelines.
out: Identify common security policies.
out: Choose the right document for a situation.
why: Security decisions must be written down and enforceable. Exam questions often test whether you can tell a policy from a standard or a procedure.
plain: A policy says what must happen: all staff must wear seatbelts. A standard gives measurable requirements: the seatbelt must meet this rating. A procedure gives the steps: buckle it this way. A guideline gives recommended advice: consider adjusting your seat first.
tech: Guidelines are recommended, not mandatory, and include benchmarks, advisories, implementation guides and reference architecture. Standards are mandatory and measurable, and include baselines, passwords, physical security, encryption and technical specifications such as a request for comments (RFC).
tech: Procedures are step-by-step instructions, such as a standard operating procedure (SOP) or a runbook. Plans include business continuity and disaster recovery. Policies set high-level expectations, including bring your own device (BYOD), acceptable use policy (AUP), clean desk, information security, incident response, data classification and retention, access control, data disposal, vulnerability disclosure and privacy.

| Policy | States what must be done | Yes
| Standard | Sets specific measurable requirements | Yes
| Procedure | Gives the steps to carry it out | Yes
| Guideline | Offers recommended approaches | No

out: Identify, assess and analyze risk.

out: Choose a treatment and maintain a risk register.
why: Security is mostly about deciding which risks matter and what to do about them, with limited money and time.
plain: Consider home insurance. You think about what you own, what could happen, how likely it is and what it would cost. Then you lock the doors, buy insurance, move out of the flood zone or simply accept the small risks.
tech: Risk identification includes asset identification and stakeholder ownership. Risk assessment uses scoring and categorization. Risk analysis considers impact, likelihood or probability, the owner, current mitigations, and whether the method is qualitative (categories like high or low) or quantitative (numbers).
tech: A risk register records risks, owners, scores and treatments, and supports communication and regular reviews. Risk treatment options are transferring (such as insurance), accepting, avoiding (stopping the activity) and mitigating (reducing the risk).
tech: Business-level considerations include a business impact analysis (BIA), which identifies critical functions and the effect of losing them; risk appetite, the amount of risk an organization will pursue; residual risk, which remains after controls; stakeholder involvement; management oversight; and regulatory and legal requirements.
tech: Quantitative risk: single loss expectancy (SLE) is asset value multiplied by exposure factor. The annualized rate of occurrence (ARO) is how many times per year an event is expected. Annualized loss expectancy (ALE) is SLE multiplied by ARO.

tech: Vendor selection uses a request for proposal (RFP), request for information (RFI), request for quote (RFQ) and expression of interest (EOI). Due diligence evaluates a vendor before signing. Conflict of interest occurs when someone's personal interests could influence the decision.
tech: Agreement types: a service-level agreement (SLA) defines measurable service commitments, and a service-level objective (SLO) is a specific target within it. tech: Vendor monitoring includes right to audit, service-level monitoring, a vendor registry, vendor assessment, compliance attestation and penetration testing. Limitations and constraints include staffing limitations, resource availability, environment, legal and regulatory factors, geography/jurisdiction, financial/return on investment (ROI), vendor lock-in and assurance mechanisms. Rules of engagement define what testers may and may not do.

out: Distinguish penetration test types and passive and active reconnaissance.
out: Use MITRE ATT&CK, the Cyber Kill Chain and the Diamond Model.
why: Audits confirm that controls actually work. Knowing the type of engagement tells you who does what, and with what independence.
plain: An audit is a health check by someone looking at your records. A penetration test is hiring a friendly burglar to try the doors. Known environment means the burglar has floor plans. Unknown environment means they have none.
tech: Data gathering uses sampling, questionnaires/surveys, interviews and assertion, which is a claim to be tested. Reference sources include MITRE ATT&CK, a knowledge base of real attacker techniques; the Cyber Kill Chain, which describes the stages of an intrusion; and the Diamond Model of Intrusion Analysis, which relates adversary, capability, infrastructure and victim. Scoping uses an audit charter, which sets authority and purpose, and frequency.
tech: Engagement types include gap analysis; internal work by compliance staff, the audit committee and self-assessments; external work, including examinations, assessments, regulatory reviews and independent third-party audits; and benchmarking against peers or standards.
tech: Penetration testing can be known environment (full details), unknown environment (no details) or partially known environment. It may be physical, offensive (attacking), defensive (defending) or integrated (both sides working together, often called purple teaming). Reconnaissance can be passive, which avoids touching the target, such as public records, or active, which interacts with the target, such as scanning.
tech: Frameworks and standards include industry-based standards such as PCI DSS, international standards such as ISO/IEC 27001, and region-specific standards such as GDPR requirements. Functional testing checks that a control works as designed, and behavioral testing checks how people or systems actually act.
