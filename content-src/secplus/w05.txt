out: Apply confidentiality, integrity and availability to a real situation.
out: Separate authentication, authorization and accounting, and explain non-repudiation.
out: Describe defense in depth, least privilege and Zero Trust principles.
why: These ideas are the vocabulary of the entire field. Every control you will study protects confidentiality, integrity or availability, and many exam scenarios simply ask which of those was harmed.
plain: Think of your medical records. Confidentiality means strangers cannot read them. Integrity means nobody can quietly change your blood type. Availability means the doctor can open them when you are in the emergency room.
plain: AAA is airport security. Authentication checks your passport, authorization checks that your ticket lets you board this flight, and accounting records that you boarded. Non-repudiation is a signed receipt: you cannot later claim it was not you.
plain: Zero Trust is like a hospital where your badge is checked at every door, not only at the entrance. Being inside the building earns no automatic trust.
tech: The CIA triad is confidentiality, integrity and availability. Encryption and access control protect confidentiality. Hashes and digital signatures detect changes, which supports integrity. Redundancy and backups support availability.
tech: AAA stands for authentication, authorization and accounting. Authentication proves identity. Authorization grants permissions. Accounting records what was done. Non-repudiation means someone cannot credibly deny having performed an action, and is commonly achieved through digital signatures tied to a private key that only that person controls.
tech: Defense in depth layers different controls so that one failure does not lead to total compromise. Least privilege gives each user or process only the access needed, for only as long as needed. NIST SP 800-207 describes Zero Trust as moving defenses away from network location and toward users, assets and resources. Principles include verifying each request, assuming breach, granting per-session least privilege and using device and context signals in decisions.
tech: In the Zero Trust architecture, a Policy Decision Point (PDP) decides whether to allow access, and a Policy Enforcement Point (PEP) carries out that decision between the subject and the resource. These terms come from NIST SP 800-207 and are useful even though the current exam list focuses on the principles.

q 1.1 mcq m | Non-repudiation | A company needs to prove that a specific manager approved a contract, in a way the manager cannot convincingly deny. What provides this?
+ A digital signature created with the manager's private key :: Only the holder of the private key can create it, which supports non-repudiation.
- A shared team password :: A shared credential cannot tie the action to one person.
- Full disk encryption :: This protects stored data but does not prove who acted.
- A symmetric key known to both parties :: Either party could have produced the result, so it cannot prove which did.

- Granting a user access to a folder :: That is authorization.
- Encrypting a user's laptop :: That is a data protection control.

- Access is granted because the employee signed in earlier today :: Access decisions are made per session and per resource.

title: Security controls: categories and types
domain: Domain 1: General Security Concepts
obj: 1.1

pre: w5-1
out: Classify any control by category and by type.
out: Explain why one control can have more than one type.
out: Recommend a compensating control when the preferred control is not possible.
why: Exam questions regularly give a control and ask you to classify it. In real work, classification shows gaps, such as an organization with plenty of prevention and no way of detecting when prevention fails.
plain: Category describes how a control is carried out: through technology, management decisions, people's daily routines or physical things. Type describes what the control is trying to achieve: stop it, discourage it, notice it, fix it, tell people what to do, or stand in when something else cannot be used.
plain: Take a house. The fence is physical, and it is preventive and deterring. The camera is physical or technical, and detective. The rule that the last person locks up is directive. Repairing a broken lock is corrective.
tech: The four control categories are technical (also called logical), which are implemented in technology such as firewalls and encryption; managerial (also called administrative), which are decisions and documentation such as policies and risk assessments; operational, which are carried out by people, such as security guards or awareness training; and physical (also called environmental), such as locks, fences and fire suppression.
tech: The six control types are preventive (stops an event), deterring (discourages an attempt), detective (identifies an event), corrective (restores after an event), compensating (an alternative when the primary control is not feasible) and directive (tells people what to do, such as a policy).
tech: Category and type are separate questions, so each control has both. Context can change the type. A guard who physically stops someone is preventive, while one simply watching monitors is detective.
table: Control examples | Control | Category | Main type
| Firewall rule blocking a port | Technical | Preventive
| Warning sign about cameras | Physical | Deterring
| Log review by an analyst | Operational | Detective
| Restoring from backup | Technical or operational | Corrective
| Fire suppression system | Physical | Corrective
| Acceptable use policy | Managerial | Directive
| Network isolation for an unpatchable machine | Technical | Compensating
| Security guard at a door | Operational | Preventive and deterring
term: Technical control == A control carried out by technology. Also called a logical control.
term: Managerial control == A control based on policy, oversight and risk decisions. Also called administrative.
term: Operational control == A control carried out by people following a routine.
term: Physical control == A tangible control, such as a lock or fence. Environmental controls such as fire suppression are grouped here.
term: Preventive == Designed to stop an event from happening.
term: Deterring == Designed to discourage someone from trying.
term: Detective == Designed to identify that something happened.
term: Corrective == Designed to fix things and restore after an event.
term: Compensating == An alternative used when the primary control cannot be applied.
term: Directive == Tells people what they must do, such as a policy.
eg: An old medical device cannot be patched. The hospital isolates it on its own network segment and monitors traffic. That is a compensating control.
eg: A company's backups are corrective, the alert that ransomware began is detective, and the rule forbidding personal USB drives is directive.

domain: Domain 1: General Security Concepts

out: Follow a change from request to documentation.
out: Identify the security impact of a technical change.
out: Choose between backing out and failing forward.
why: Unplanned changes cause outages and open security gaps. Many incidents begin with an undocumented firewall exception or a rushed update. Good change management turns risky work into controlled work.
plain: Think of road works. Someone asks permission, planners check what roads will be affected, and they set a time when traffic is light. They also plan what to do if it goes wrong, then update the maps when it is finished. Change management applies the same discipline to technology.
tech: In the business process, an owner is accountable for the change. Stakeholders are the people affected. An impact analysis estimates what could go wrong, including security effects. Test results show that the change worked in a safe environment. A Change Advisory Board (CAB) reviews significant changes through an approval process. Standard operating procedures (SOPs) describe how routine work is done.
tech: A maintenance window is a period agreed in advance when work can happen with minimal disruption. Backout planning is the prepared way to return to the earlier state. Fail forward is the alternative: when going back is riskier or impossible, you fix the problem and keep moving ahead, such as after a database migration that cannot be cleanly reversed.
tech: Technical implications include: changing allow lists or deny lists, so that a new application is not blocked or a dangerous one permitted; restricted activities, meaning actions that are prohibited during the work; downtime; a service restart or application restart; legacy applications, which may break under new settings; and dependencies, where changing one component affects another.
tech: After the change, documentation must catch up: updating diagrams and updating policies and procedures. Version control tracks each revision of code, configurations and documents so that you can see who changed what and return to a previous state.
table: A change record | Stage | Key question | Security angle
| Request and ownership | Who is accountable? | Clear responsibility
| Impact analysis | What could break? | Does this expose anything or remove a control?
| Testing | Did it work in a safe place? | Do the test results include security checks?
| CAB approval | Should we proceed? | Security represented at the review
| Implementation | Is it in the maintenance window? | Restricted activities followed
| Backout or fail forward | What if it goes wrong? | The system is not left half-protected
| Documentation | Are records current? | Diagrams and procedures match reality

title: Cryptography: encryption, hashing, salting and signatures
domain: Domain 1: General Security Concepts
obj: 1.3
level: Intermediate
min: 120
pre: w5-1, w3-3
out: Compare symmetric and asymmetric encryption, and choose for a use case.
out: Distinguish encryption, hashing, salting and obfuscation.
out: Explain digital signatures and key exchange.
why: Cryptography protects data on disks, on networks and in signatures. Most failures are not broken mathematics. They are poor choices of algorithm, weak key handling or using the wrong tool, such as encrypting passwords when they should be hashed.
plain: Encryption is a lockbox: with the key you can get the original back. Hashing is a blender: you can make a smoothie from fruit, but cannot turn it back. If two people blend the same fruit, they get the same smoothie, which is how you can check that something has not changed.
plain: In symmetric encryption, both people share the same key, like a shared house key. In asymmetric encryption, there is a pair: a public key that anyone can use to lock a box, and a private key that only you can use to open it.
tech: Symmetric algorithms use one shared key and are fast, so they are used for bulk data. The Advanced Encryption Standard (AES) is the main example, with key lengths of 128, 192 or 256 bits. Asymmetric algorithms use a public and private key pair, and are slower, so they are used for key exchange and signatures. RSA and elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) are examples. ECC gets comparable strength from much shorter keys. For the same algorithm, a longer key length generally makes brute force harder.
tech: Key exchange solves the problem of agreeing on a shared key over an open network. Diffie-Hellman, including its elliptic curve form, lets two parties derive a shared secret without ever sending it. TLS uses asymmetric methods to agree on a key, and symmetric encryption for the session. That is the transport/communication use of encryption. Forward secrecy uses temporary keys, so stealing a long-term key later cannot unlock past sessions.
tech: A hashing algorithm produces a fixed-length digest from any input. It is one-way, and any small change in the input yields a very different digest. SHA-256 is a current choice, whereas MD5 and SHA-1 are considered broken for collision resistance. A salt is a random value added to each password before hashing, so identical passwords produce different hashes and pre-computed rainbow tables are defeated. Passwords should be stored with slow, purpose-built functions such as bcrypt, scrypt or Argon2.
tech: A digital signature is made by hashing a message and signing that hash with the sender's private key. Anyone can verify it with the sender's public key, which gives integrity, authentication of origin and non-repudiation. Obfuscation makes something hard to understand without protecting it mathematically. Steganography, hiding data within other data, and code minification are examples. Obfuscation is not encryption.
tech: Encryption can be applied at different levels: full disk (the entire drive), partition, volume (a logical storage unit), file, database, or an individual record. The narrower the level, the more precisely you can control access, and the more there is to manage. Protocols such as TLS, IPsec and SSH apply encryption in transit.
tech: Cryptographic tools include the Trusted Platform Module (TPM), a chip in a computer that stores keys and supports full disk encryption; the hardware security module (HSM), a dedicated device that protects keys at scale; key management systems, which handle generating, storing and rotating keys; and the secure enclave, an isolated processor area that protects sensitive operations.

term: Asymmetric encryption == Encryption using a matched public and private key pair.

term: RSA == A widely used asymmetric algorithm.

term: Diffie-Hellman == A key exchange method that derives a shared secret without sending it.
term: Forward secrecy == A property that protects past sessions if a long-term key is later stolen.
term: Hashing algorithm == A one-way function that produces a fixed-length digest.
term: Salting == Adding a unique random value to a password before hashing.
term: Digital signature == A hash signed with a private key, verifiable with the matching public key.
term: Obfuscation == Making information hard to understand without cryptographic protection.
term: Steganography == Hiding data inside other data, such as an image.
term: Full disk encryption == Encrypting an entire drive.
term: Partition == A defined section of a physical disk that can be encrypted separately.
term: Volume == A logical storage area that can be encrypted as a unit.
term: File encryption == Encrypting individual files.
term: Database encryption == Encrypting data at the database level.
term: Record encryption == Encrypting individual records or fields.
term: Transport encryption == Encrypting communication as it travels across a network.
term: Protocols == Agreed rules for communication, such as TLS, IPsec and SSH.
term: TPM == Trusted Platform Module: a chip that stores keys on a device.
term: HSM == Hardware security module: a dedicated device for protecting keys.
term: Tools == Cryptographic tools include TPMs, HSMs, key management systems and secure enclaves.
term: Secure enclave == An isolated processor area for sensitive operations.
eg: BitLocker can use a laptop's TPM, so the drive decrypts only on the original hardware in its expected state.
eg: When a password database leaks, salted bcrypt hashes make cracking far slower than unsalted MD5.
walk: Choose the right tool
- Need to keep a large file secret and get it back? Use symmetric encryption, such as AES.
- Need to share a key with someone you have never met? Use asymmetric key exchange.
- Need to store passwords? Use a salted, slow hash such as Argon2, not encryption.
- Need to detect whether a download changed? Compare a SHA-256 hash.
- Need to prove who approved something? Use a digital signature.

scenario: A developer proposes encrypting user passwords with AES and storing the key on the same server, so that passwords can be recovered for users. What should be recommended?

q 1.3 mcq e | Symmetric versus asymmetric | Which statement is true about symmetric encryption?
+ The same key encrypts and decrypts :: That is the defining feature.
- It uses a public and private key pair :: That describes asymmetric encryption.
- It cannot be used for large files :: Symmetric encryption is well suited to bulk data.
- It is one-way and cannot be reversed :: That describes hashing.

- AES encryption :: Encryption provides confidentiality, not a simple integrity check.
- Obfuscation :: Obfuscation does not verify anything.
- A longer password :: A password has no bearing on file integrity.
q 1.3 mcq m | Salting | What is the main benefit of salting password hashes?
+ Identical passwords produce different hashes :: Unique salts defeat pre-computed tables.
- Hashes can be reversed more easily :: Salting does not make hashes reversible.
- Passwords no longer need to be long :: Weak passwords remain weak.
- The salt must be kept as secret as a private key :: Salts are normally stored with the hash and need not be secret.
q 1.3 mcq h | Digital signatures | Which key does a sender use to create a digital signature?

- The sender's public key :: Public keys verify signatures.
- The recipient's public key :: This would be used to encrypt data for the recipient.
- A shared symmetric key :: A shared key cannot prove which party acted.

out: Explain how a certificate chain is validated.
out: Compare CRL and OCSP, and self-signed and third-party certificates.
out: Generate a certificate signing request.
why: Certificates are how you know you are talking to your real bank and not an impostor. When a certificate expires or a key is stolen, services go down or trust breaks. PKI failures are a common cause of outages as well as attacks.
plain: A certificate is an ID card for a website or person. A certificate authority is the government office that issues it. You trust the ID because you trust the office. Public key infrastructure is the whole system of offices, ID cards and lists of cancelled cards.
tech: Public key infrastructure (PKI) is the set of people, policies and systems that create, manage and revoke certificates. A certificate binds a public key to an identity and is signed by a certificate authority (CA). The private key stays secret with the owner. The public key may be shared. Key escrow means a trusted third party holds a copy of a key so that it can be recovered, for example if an employee leaves or loses it. Escrowed keys need strict controls.
tech: A root of trust is the starting point that is trusted by default. Browsers and operating systems ship with root CA certificates. A root CA usually signs an intermediate CA, and the intermediate CA signs end-entity certificates. To validate, the client checks the chain up to a trusted root, the name, the validity dates and the revocation status.
tech: To request a certificate, you generate a key pair and create a certificate signing request (CSR), which contains the public key and identifying details. The CA verifies the request and returns a signed certificate. The private key is never sent. A wildcard certificate covers all first-level subdomains of a domain, such as *.example.com. A Subject Alternative Name (SAN) lists additional names. A third-party certificate comes from a public CA and is broadly trusted. A self-signed certificate is signed by its own key, and clients will not trust it automatically, so it suits internal testing.
tech: Revocation is used when a certificate must be withdrawn early, for example because its key was stolen. A certificate revocation list (CRL) is a signed list of revoked serial numbers that a client downloads. The Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) lets a client ask about one certificate in real time. OCSP stapling lets the server attach a recent signed OCSP response.

eg: A website's certificate expires over a weekend, so browsers warn all visitors and sales stop. Many organizations now automate renewal.

walk: How your browser trusts a site
- The server sends its certificate and any intermediate certificates.
- The browser checks that the name matches the address and that the certificate is within its dates.
- It verifies each signature up the chain until it reaches a root it already trusts.
- It checks revocation status using OCSP or CRL data.
- If all pass, the handshake continues. If not, you see a warning.

scenario: A web server's private key is stolen, but its certificate has 200 days of validity remaining. What should be done, and how will clients learn of it?
