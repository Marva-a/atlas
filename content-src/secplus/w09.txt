out: Pick a protection method to fit the need.
out: Distinguish masking, tokenization and encryption.
why: Data is usually what attackers are after. Knowing what you have and what state it is in determines how to protect it.
plain: Think of money. In a vault it is at rest. In an armored truck it is in transit. On the counter while a teller counts it, it is in use. Each state needs its own protection.
tech: Structured data has a defined format, such as database tables. Unstructured data has none, such as documents, images and email. Data at rest is stored. Data in transit moves across a network. Data in use is being processed in memory, which is hardest to protect. Classifications include public, sensitive, confidential, restricted, critical, and in government settings secret and top secret.
tech: Methods: masking hides part of a value, such as showing only the last four digits. Hashing creates a one-way digest. Filtering removes or blocks certain data. Tokenization replaces a sensitive value with a random token, stored in a separate vault. Encryption transforms data so that a key is required. Data transpose rearranges characters or fields, a weak method by itself. De-identification (deidentification) removes information that links data to a person. Obfuscation makes data harder to interpret without strong protection.

| Hashing | No | Passwords and integrity
| Tokenization | Yes, through the vault | Payment card systems
| Encryption | Yes, with the key | Files and traffic
| De-identification | Should not be | Research data sets

why: When everyone is responsible, no one is. Clearly assigned data roles and known life-cycle stages are the backbone of privacy obligations.
plain: A library has an owner who sets the rules, a librarian who looks after the books and an assistant who checks them out. Data has similar roles. Data also has a life, from being created until it is securely destroyed.
tech: Data protection roles: the data owner is accountable for the data and decides how it is classified. The data custodian implements and maintains the technical controls. The data steward looks after data quality and proper use. The data operator handles day-to-day processing. The data controller decides why and how personal data is processed. A data subprocessor is engaged by a processor to carry out part of the processing.
tech: Data handling covers: endpoints, where data is often copied and lost; marking and labeling, which show classification; geofencing, which restricts access by location; data location, where data physically resides; and data placement, deciding where data is stored according to its sensitivity.
tech: The data management life cycle covers creation, management, distribution, retention and disposal. Data compliance is shaped by standards and by the kind of data: health data, personal information, financial data, child/minor data, intellectual property and legal data. Examples include HIPAA for US health data, GDPR in the EU and PIPEDA in Canada, and PCI DSS for payment card data.

out: Compare hot, warm and cold sites.
out: Choose redundancy strategies.
out: Select the right power protection.
why: Availability is one-third of the CIA triad. Ransomware, storms and hardware failure all test whether a business can keep working.
plain: A hot site is a furnished, stocked spare office you can walk into right away. A warm site has furniture but needs setup. A cold site is an empty room with power and a lease. Redundancy is keeping a spare tire, and load balancing is sharing the weight among several tires.
tech: Site considerations: a hot site is fully equipped and current, offering fast recovery at high cost. A warm site has hardware but needs current data and setup. A cold site has space and utilities only. Environmental factors mean a recovery site should not share the same flood plain, power grid or earthquake zone.
tech: Platform diversity reduces common-mode failure through a different vendor platform, different hardware or different virtualization technology.
tech: Redundancy strategies: load balancing spreads requests across servers. Clustering groups servers so that one can take over from another. Autoscaling adds or removes capacity automatically. High availability means designing to minimize downtime. Multicloud systems use multiple providers. Storage redundancy includes RAID and replication. Power protection includes an uninterruptible power supply (UPS) for short-term battery power, a redundant power supply (RPS) in a device, a power generator for long outages and a surge protector for voltage spikes.

title: Backups, testing, disaster recovery and recovery metrics
domain: Domain 3: Security Architecture
obj: 3.4
level: Intermediate
min: 120
pre: w9-3
out: Design a backup approach that resists ransomware.
out: Define and calculate RTO, RPO, MTTR and MTBF.
out: Distinguish disaster recovery, business continuity and testing types.
why: A backup that has never been restored is an assumption. Recovery metrics turn vague hopes into commitments that a business can plan and pay for.
plain: RPO is how far back you can afford to go: if the last save was four hours ago, you lose four hours of work. RTO is how long you can afford to be down. MTBF is how long something tends to work before breaking, and MTTR is how long it takes to fix.
tech: Backups involve retention (how long copies are kept), immutability (copies cannot be altered or deleted for a period, which resists ransomware), scope (what is included, such as full, incremental or differential) and restoration testing (proving a backup can be recovered). A common guideline is three copies, on two media, with one offsite.
tech: Testing types: failover moves work to a standby system. Simulation walks through a scenario without affecting production. Parallel processing runs recovery systems alongside production to compare results. Disaster recovery focuses on restoring technology after a major event. Business continuity keeps the critical functions of the organization running. Capacity planning ensures that people, technology and infrastructure can meet expected demand.
tech: Recovery metrics: the recovery time objective (RTO) is the maximum acceptable time to restore a service. The recovery point objective (RPO) is the maximum acceptable data loss measured in time. Mean time to repair (MTTR) is the average time to fix a failure. Mean time between failures (MTBF) is the average operating time between failures. MTBF equals total operating time divided by the number of failures.
diagram: rpo-rto
table: Recovery metrics | Metric | Asks | Example
| RPO | How much data can we lose? | Backups every 4 hours give an RPO of up to 4 hours
| RTO | How long can we be down? | Service back within 2 hours
| MTTR | How long does a repair take on average? | 3 repairs took 6 hours in total, so 2 hours
| MTBF | How long between failures on average? | 1,000 hours and 4 failures, so 250 hours
term: Backups == Copies of data kept for recovery.
term: Retention == How long backup copies are kept.
term: Immutability == Backups that cannot be altered for a set period.
term: Scope == What a backup includes.
term: Restoration testing == Proving that backups can be recovered.
term: Failover == Moving work to a standby system.
term: Simulation == Practising a scenario without affecting production.
term: Parallel processing == Running recovery systems alongside production to compare results.
term: Disaster recovery == Restoring technology after a major event.
term: Business continuity == Keeping critical functions running during disruption.
term: Capacity planning == Making sure resources can meet expected demand.
term: RTO == Recovery time objective: maximum acceptable downtime.
term: RPO == Recovery point objective: maximum acceptable data loss, in time.
term: MTTR == Mean time to repair: the average time to fix a failure.
term: MTBF == Mean time between failures: average operating time between failures.
eg: Ransomware encrypts file servers, but immutable backups cannot be changed, so the business restores from them without paying.
