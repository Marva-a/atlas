Practice: design an agent permission and approval experience

Pathway: AI Security · Lesson 6 · Advanced · about 230 min

Bring the pathway together by growing your Day 51 approval screen into a full experience: how an admin grants an enterprise AI agent its authority, how users approve its consequential actions and how both can see, limit and revoke what it does.

By the end, you can: Design and present a tested prototype of an agent permission and approval experience, explaining how it limits prompt injection, excessive agency and data exposure, and labelling honestly what was simulated.

Why it matters to a designer

This is a portfolio piece that very few designers can show yet: a technically grounded agent access experience. It connects identity (Weeks 1 and 4), least privilege (Week 7), machine identities (Day 50) and agent security (Day 51 and this pathway) in one design.

Key ideas

  • Scope the problem: one agent with a real job (for example an IT helpdesk agent that can read tickets, reset MFA and change group membership) in one company.
  • Three surfaces to design, starting from the approval screen you sketched on Day 51: admin setup (which tools and scopes the agent gets and who may use it), the user's in-the-moment approvals, and the oversight views (activity timeline, inventory, revoke).
  • Use risk tiers: read-only actions run freely; reversible changes run with a visible log; consequential or irreversible actions need confirmation; some actions are never allowed for an agent at all.
  • Design against approval fatigue: fewer, clearer prompts for the actions that matter, details computed by the system and not just described by the model, and hard limits behind every approval.
  • Test with a realistic participant (an IT admin or security engineer if you can reach one): give them an injected ticket and watch whether your design helps them notice and stop a harmful action.
  • Label it honestly: self-initiated, simulated data, what you tested and with whom. State what you would need to confirm with engineers.

Key terms

risk tierA level of checking matched to how harmful an action could be.
oversight viewA screen where people review what an agent has done and can intervene.
revocationTaking away access that was granted, ideally effective immediately.

Today's steps

Choose the agent, its job and its five or six tools. Write the risk tier for each tool action.

20 min

Sketch the admin setup flow: adding the agent, choosing tools and scopes, choosing who may use it, and setting hard limits.

40 min

Rework your Day 51 approval screen into two in-the-moment approvals, one for a reversible change and one for an irreversible one, including what the user sees when an action is blocked by a hard limit.

40 min

Design the oversight view: an agent inventory row and an activity timeline with a pause and revoke control.

30 min

Make a clickable prototype of the approval and oversight screens and test it with one person, using a scenario where a ticket contains an injected instruction. Note what they noticed and missed.

60 min

Write a short case note: the problem, the risks you designed against, the design, what testing showed, honest labels and next steps. Share it with one practitioner for feedback.

40 min

Skip or skim

Skip polishing visual details and building a working agent. A clear, tested prototype with sound reasoning about authority and risk is the goal.

Check yourself

  • Which agent actions did you place in each risk tier, and why?
  • How does your design still protect the company if a user approves a harmful action?
  • What did your test participant miss, and what did you change because of it?

What a good answer looks like

  • For example: reading tickets is read-only and runs freely; adding a note is reversible and logged; resetting MFA needs confirmation; adding someone to an admin group is never allowed for the agent. The reasoning should follow impact and reversibility.
  • Through hard limits that hold regardless of approval, such as never granting admin roles, rate limits on resets, and allow-listed groups, plus a full activity log and an immediate pause and revoke control.
  • An honest answer names a specific miss, such as not noticing the target account in a reset confirmation, and a specific change, such as showing the account's name and role prominently and requiring the user to type it for high-risk resets.
