Indicators of malicious activity: malware, physical and social engineering

Lesson 27 · Week 7 · Domain 2: Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks · Objective 2.5 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Tell malware types apart by their behaviour.
  • Recognize physical attacks.
  • Identify types of social engineering from a scenario.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Scenario questions describe symptoms and ask what is happening. Being able to go from a symptom to a named attack is exactly the skill an analyst uses each day.

In plain language

Malware is software built to do harm. Different kinds behave differently, the way a pickpocket, an arsonist and a blackmailer are all criminals but act differently. Social engineering skips the technology altogether and tricks the person.

2. Technical deep dive

Ransomware encrypts data and demands payment. A trojan looks legitimate but hides harmful functions. A worm spreads by itself across networks. A virus attaches to a file and needs someone to run it. Spyware collects information secretly. Adware displays unwanted advertising. A rootkit hides itself and others deep in the system, often with high privileges. A keylogger records keystrokes. A logic bomb waits for a condition, such as a date or an account being deleted. Fileless malware runs in memory or through built-in tools, leaving few files on disk.

Physical attacks include tailgating (following an authorized person through a door), shoulder surfing (watching someone enter information), skimming (a hidden device reads card data) and forced entry (breaking in).

Social engineering attacks include phishing by email, spear phishing aimed at a specific person, whaling aimed at senior executives, smishing by text message, vishing by voice, and quishing using QR codes. Impersonation is pretending to be someone trusted. A deepfake is AI-generated audio or video that imitates a real person.

Walkthrough: Identify malware from symptoms

  1. Symptom: thousands of hosts infected in an hour, and no one clicked anything. That suggests a worm.
  2. Symptom: a user downloaded a free PDF tool, and later data left the host. That suggests a trojan.
  3. Symptom: security tools show nothing, but the system behaves abnormally. Consider a rootkit.
  4. Symptom: damage appeared at midnight on the last day of the month. Consider a logic bomb.

3. See it in real products

Social engineering targets the humans around a product. An attacker calls support claiming to be locked out, or impersonates an administrator in a message. Identity-verification scripts, support tools and internal consoles are product surfaces, and they decide whether a good story is enough to get in.

In security operations

  • An employee receives a call in the CEO's cloned voice asking for a wire transfer. It combines vishing, impersonation and a deepfake.
  • A logic bomb written by a departing developer deletes records once the developer's account has been removed.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design a support flow that resists impersonation

About 45 min

Scenario: A healthcare portal's support team can reset a patient's email address after hearing a date of birth and an address, both easy to find. An impersonation attempt recently succeeded.

Problem statement: The internal support tool gives agents one form with no verification steps, under pressure to close tickets quickly.

Your design task:

  • Map how a caller's identity is verified today, and where a convincing story can win.
  • Redesign the support console for email changes, with graded verification.
  • Write the script and the interface for an agent who must refuse.
  • Design the notification sent to the existing email and phone.

Technical security constraints:

  • Knowledge-based facts are weak, because they can be found or bought.
  • Agents must not be able to complete a high-risk change alone.
  • Patients who have lost their phone and email still need a legitimate route.

User needs:

  • Agents need permission to say no without being penalized.
  • Patients in real difficulty need a route that still works.
  • Security staff need a record of each override.

Expected deliverable: A redesigned support console flow, an agent refusal script and notification copy.

Evaluation criteria:

  • High-risk changes need stronger proof than publicly discoverable facts.
  • The design anticipates urgency, authority and sympathy as pressure tactics.
  • The console makes the correct next step clear for an agent mid-call.
  • Support metrics are considered, since speed targets can reward unsafe behaviour.

Senior-level considerations:

  • Internal tools deserve real design attention, because they hold the most power and often get the least care.
  • Propose changes to how agents are measured, as an interface alone will not outweigh a target.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • What can support staff change in an account without additional approval?

Security:

  • What is our verification standard for an account recovery call?

Product:

  • How long may support take on a verification before customers complain?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A finance employee receives a text message with a link about a suspended bank account. Later the same day, an executive is targeted by an email carefully written around a real upcoming acquisition. Name the two attacks.

Compare your reasoning

The first is smishing, since it arrives by SMS. The second is whaling: spear phishing tailored to a senior executive.

1. Which malware spreads without user action?

  • Worm
  • Virus
  • Trojan
  • Adware
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Worm. Worms replicate on their own.
  • Incorrect: Virus. A virus needs an infected file to be run.
  • Incorrect: Trojan. A trojan relies on the user installing it.
  • Incorrect: Adware. Adware does not generally self-replicate.

2. A caller claims to be from IT and asks for a password reset code. What is this attack?

  • Vishing
  • Smishing
  • Quishing
  • Tailgating
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Vishing. It is social engineering by voice.
  • Incorrect: Smishing. Smishing is by text message.
  • Incorrect: Quishing. Quishing uses QR codes.
  • Incorrect: Tailgating. Tailgating is physical.

3. A script deletes payroll files on the first day after its author's account is disabled. What is this?

  • Logic bomb
  • Keylogger
  • Worm
  • Adware
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Logic bomb. It triggers on a condition.
  • Incorrect: Keylogger. A keylogger records typing.
  • Incorrect: Worm. Nothing indicates it spreads.
  • Incorrect: Adware. It displays adverts.

4. Antivirus finds no malicious files, but PowerShell is making repeated outbound connections and running code in memory. What is the most likely malware type?

  • Fileless malware
  • Adware
  • A virus attached to a document
  • Skimming
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Fileless malware. It operates in memory and through built-in tools.
  • Incorrect: Adware. Adware displays adverts and is usually visible.
  • Incorrect: A virus attached to a document. This would normally leave file-based evidence.
  • Incorrect: Skimming. This is a card-data attack.

5. Match each description to the attack.

Correct matches
  • Following a colleague through a badge door: Tailgating
  • Watching a PIN being typed: Shoulder surfing
  • A hidden card reader on an ATM: Skimming
  • Breaking a door lock: Forced entry

Each describes how the physical barrier was defeated.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. A caller is upset and urges an agent to skip a verification step because of a medical emergency. What should the design support?

  • An escalation path that keeps verification in place
  • A button that lets the agent waive verification on request
  • Hiding all verification from agents to make calls faster
  • Ending any call in which the caller becomes upset
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: An escalation path that keeps verification in place. Urgency is a common tactic, so a safe route must exist for real emergencies.
  • Incorrect: A button that lets the agent waive verification on request. This turns any convincing story into a bypass.
  • Incorrect: Hiding all verification from agents to make calls faster. Agents need to see and perform verification for it to work.
  • Incorrect: Ending any call in which the caller becomes upset. Genuine patients in distress would be left with no help at all.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up virus and worm. Ask whether it needs a person to run it.
  • Thinking whaling is simply big phishing. It targets senior leaders.

Summary

  • Identify malware by what it does.
  • Physical attacks defeat barriers and observation.
  • Social engineering varies by channel and target.
