Threat vectors part 1: messages, files, browsers, networks and remote access

Lesson 24 · Week 6 · Domain 2: Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks · Objective 2.3 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Identify message, image, attachment and browser-based vectors.
  • Explain network-based and remote access vectors.
  • Describe an advanced persistent threat.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

A vector is the route an attacker uses to get in. If you can name the route, you can choose a control for it.

In plain language

A threat vector is the way in: the unlocked door, the delivery driver, the open window. An advanced persistent threat is a burglar who moves in quietly and lives in your attic for months.

2. Technical deep dive

An advanced persistent threat (APT) is a skilled, well-funded attacker that keeps long-term, stealthy access to a target. Message-based vectors include email, Short Message Service (SMS), Rich Communication Services (RCS), instant messaging and collaboration tools such as shared chat platforms. All can deliver links or impersonation.

Image-based vectors include quick response (QR) codes that hide a malicious link, embedded content such as images with tracking or hidden data, and tricks involving CAPTCHA. A CAPTCHA, or Completely Automated Public Turing test to tell Computers and Humans Apart, can be imitated on fake pages, which then instruct people to paste and run commands.

Attachment-based vectors include embedded macros in office documents, Rich Text Format (RTF) documents and Portable Document Format (PDF) files that exploit a reader or contain malicious links. Browser-based vectors include malicious extensions; JavaScript running in a page; weaknesses involving password managers, such as fake autofill forms; stolen cookies; and theft of session tokens, which can let an attacker act as a signed-in user without knowing the password.

Network-based vectors include attacks on infrastructure devices such as routers and firewalls, attacks on virtualized devices and hypervisors, and capture of session keys. Remote access vectors include remote desktop services exposed to the internet, Virtual Network Computing (VNC) and virtual private network (VPN) gateways, which are frequent targets of credential attacks and unpatched flaws.

Walkthrough: Identify the vector

  1. Read: an employee opened a Word file from email and enabled content, and malware ran.
  2. The delivery route was email, a message-based vector.
  3. The carrier was a document with macros, an attachment-based vector.
  4. Controls: mail filtering, macro blocking and user training.
  5. Note that real attacks often combine several vectors.

Hands-on lab

Dissect a phishing message safely

About 35 min. CISA guidance and a screenshot, no live links

  1. Read CISA's guidance on recognizing phishing.
  2. Find a phishing example in your own spam folder, without clicking. View the sender's full address and hover to preview any links.
  3. List each suspicious indicator and the vector used.
  4. Do not open attachments or click links. Delete the message or report it through your provider.

Expected: A list of at least four indicators, including sender domain and link destination.

Inspecting a message without interacting with it is the safe way to practise recognizing it.

Done when:

  • You identified at least four indicators.
  • You named the vector and one control for it.

3. See it in real products

Products are delivery channels for attacks. A shared file link, an invitation email or a comment notification can all be turned into phishing, because they arrive looking trustworthy. Product design decides how much of an attacker's content can appear inside your trusted notification.

In security operations

  • A QR code sticker placed over a real one on a parking meter sends drivers to a fake payment page.
  • Attackers steal a session cookie from an infected laptop and replay it, bypassing the password and MFA already completed by the user.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Redesign a file-share notification to resist phishing

About 45 min

Scenario: Attackers create free accounts on a document-sharing product and send invitations containing fraudulent text. The emails come from the product's legitimate domain, so they pass filters and appear trustworthy.

Problem statement: The share email includes the sender's custom message and display name unfiltered, and recipients land on a page with no signal that the sender is external.

Your design task:

  • Identify every attacker-controlled field in the current email and landing page.
  • Redesign the email and the landing page.
  • Design a one-step way to report a suspicious share, and show what happens next.

Technical security constraints:

  • Attacker-supplied text must not look as though it came from the product.
  • Links must go to your own domain, with a preview before any download.
  • Sign-up is open, so sender identity cannot be assumed to be genuine.

User needs:

  • Recipients need to judge whether a share is expected.
  • Legitimate senders still need to add context.
  • Trust and safety staff need useful reports.

Expected deliverable: Redesigned email, landing page and report flow, with attacker-controlled fields annotated.

Evaluation criteria:

  • Attacker-controlled fields are visually separated from product-authored content.
  • The design considers display-name spoofing and links inside custom messages.
  • A recipient can see who shared the file, and whether they are external, within seconds.
  • The design weighs the abuse reduction against loss of legitimate sharing.

Senior-level considerations:

  • Fixing abuse often means changing a growth feature, so bring the evidence and the trade-off to the product owner.
  • Rate limits and sender reputation sit behind the design, so agree who owns them.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Which user-entered text is included in our outbound emails?

Security:

  • Have our invitations or share emails been abused for phishing?

Product:

  • Which external sharing defaults do customers depend on?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: Users of an internal chat platform receive a message from a compromised colleague's account, containing a link to a fake sign-in page. Attackers then use stolen session tokens. Which vectors are involved?

Compare your reasoning

A message-based vector using a collaboration tool delivered the link, and a browser-based vector, session token theft, enabled the account takeover.

1. A fake QR code on a poster sends users to a malicious website. Which vector category is this?

  • Image-based
  • Physical lock and key
  • Signal-based
  • Supply chain
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Image-based. QR codes are listed with image-based vectors.
  • Incorrect: Physical lock and key. No lock is involved.
  • Incorrect: Signal-based. This does not use radio signals to attack.
  • Incorrect: Supply chain. No third party is involved.

2. An attacker reuses a stolen session token and does not need a password. Why does this work?

  • The token shows that authentication already took place
  • Session tokens contain the user's password in plain text
  • Session tokens disable MFA permanently
  • Session tokens are private keys
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: The token shows that authentication already took place. The server accepts the token as proof of a signed-in session.
  • Incorrect: Session tokens contain the user's password in plain text. Tokens are not supposed to contain passwords.
  • Incorrect: Session tokens disable MFA permanently. They do not change MFA settings.
  • Incorrect: Session tokens are private keys. A token is not a private key.

3. A document asks the user to enable content before it can be viewed. What is the likely threat?

  • Embedded macros
  • A CVSS vector
  • Bluetooth tracking
  • An access vestibule
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Embedded macros. Enabling content lets macros run.
  • Incorrect: A CVSS vector. This describes a vulnerability's scoring.
  • Incorrect: Bluetooth tracking. The scenario has no wireless element.
  • Incorrect: An access vestibule. That is a physical control.

4. Which characteristic best fits an advanced persistent threat?

  • Long-term stealthy access by a capable, well-resourced attacker
  • A single opportunistic attack with a free tool
  • An accidental email to the wrong address
  • A brief website defacement for attention
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Long-term stealthy access by a capable, well-resourced attacker. Persistence and stealth define it.
  • Incorrect: A single opportunistic attack with a free tool. This describes low-skill activity.
  • Incorrect: An accidental email to the wrong address. This is an unintentional insider.
  • Incorrect: A brief website defacement for attention. This is short-lived and public.

5. Which situation is a significant remote access exposure?

  • Remote desktop open directly to the internet
  • Remote desktop reachable only through an MFA-protected gateway
  • A printer on an internal subnet
  • An isolated test VM with no network
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Remote desktop open directly to the internet. This is a common target for password attacks and exploits.
  • Incorrect: Remote desktop reachable only through an MFA-protected gateway. This reduces exposure.
  • Incorrect: A printer on an internal subnet. This is not a remote access path.
  • Incorrect: An isolated test VM with no network. It cannot be reached remotely.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. Which single change most reduces abuse of a share email that includes a sender's message?

  • Labelling the message as written by the sender
  • Adding the company logo above the sender's message
  • Making the message font larger and more readable
  • Sending the email again a day later as a reminder
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Labelling the message as written by the sender. This separates attacker-written text from the product's own voice.
  • Incorrect: Adding the company logo above the sender's message. This lends the attacker's text even more credibility.
  • Incorrect: Making the message font larger and more readable. This makes the attacker's content more prominent.
  • Incorrect: Sending the email again a day later as a reminder. This repeats the attack and increases exposure.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Assuming MFA protects a session after sign-in. Stolen session tokens can bypass it.
  • Trusting a message because it comes from a known colleague. Accounts can be compromised.

Summary

  • Message, image and attachment vectors target people.
  • Browser vectors target sessions and stored data.
  • Network and remote access vectors target exposed services.
