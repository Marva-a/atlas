Signing and encrypting email, DNS and calls: S/MIME, DNSSEC and SRTP

Lesson 22 · Week 5 · Domain 1: General Security Concepts · Objective 1.3 · Intermediate · about 100 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Explain what S/MIME, DNSSEC and SRTP each protect, and what each leaves unprotected.
  • Tell DNSSEC apart from encrypted DNS, and S/MIME apart from SPF, DKIM and DMARC.
  • Choose the right secure protocol for an email, DNS or voice and video scenario.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

TLS protects traffic while it moves between two machines. Some data needs protection that travels with it: an email that must prove who wrote it after it lands in an inbox, a DNS answer that must be genuine, a call that must stay private. These three protocols apply the signatures and certificates from the last two lessons to exactly those cases, and they come up in exam questions that ask you to pick the secure replacement for an insecure protocol.

In plain language

TLS is an armoured truck: the letter is safe on the road, but once delivered it is just a letter again. S/MIME is a wax seal and a locked envelope on the letter itself, so it stays sealed in the mailbox. DNSSEC is a notary stamp on the phone book: anyone can read the entry, but you can tell if someone changed it. SRTP is a scrambler on a phone line, so a listener hears noise.

2. Technical deep dive

Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions (S/MIME) signs and encrypts email using X.509 certificates. To sign, the sender uses their private key, and any recipient checks the signature with the sender's certificate: this gives integrity, authentication of the sender and non-repudiation. To encrypt, the sender uses each recipient's public key, so only holders of the matching private keys can read the body and attachments. Both people need certificates, usually issued by an organization's certificate authority. Message headers such as the subject line are often not protected. Pretty Good Privacy (PGP) offers the same kind of protection with a web of trust between users instead of certificate authorities.

S/MIME is end to end: it protects one message from sender to recipient. SPF, DKIM and DMARC, from Week 10, are domain-level checks done by mail servers. They tell a receiving server whether mail really came from a domain's approved systems, but they do not encrypt anything, and they say nothing about which person wrote the message.

Domain Name System Security Extensions (DNSSEC) adds digital signatures to DNS records. A zone signs its records, and each signature is published as an RRSIG record. The zone's public key is published as a DNSKEY record, and the parent zone vouches for that key with a DS (delegation signer) record. A validating resolver follows this chain of trust from the root down, and rejects answers whose signatures do not check out. DNSSEC gives integrity and origin authentication, which defeats cache poisoning. It does not give confidentiality: anyone watching can still see which names you look up. Encrypted DNS, such as DNS over HTTPS (DoH) and DNS over TLS (DoT), hides lookups from observers but does not prove that the answer is genuine. The two solve different problems and can be used together.

Secure Real-time Transport Protocol (SRTP) protects the media of voice and video calls carried by RTP. It encrypts the audio or video, authenticates each packet and protects against replay of captured packets. The call setup, usually done with the Session Initiation Protocol (SIP), is protected separately, for example with SIP over TLS, and the keys for SRTP are exchanged during that setup. Browser-based calling commonly negotiates SRTP keys using DTLS, a version of TLS for datagrams.

Key terms

PGPPretty Good Privacy: email signing and encryption based on a web of trust between users.
DNSSECDNS Security Extensions: digital signatures on DNS records, checked by validating resolvers.

DNSKEYA DNSSEC record holding a zone's public key.

Walkthrough: Choose the secure protocol for a scenario

  1. Name what must be protected: one message, a name lookup, or live media.
  2. Ask whether the need is privacy, proof of authenticity, or both.
  3. Email that must prove its author or stay private in the mailbox: S/MIME, or PGP.
  4. DNS answers that must be genuine: DNSSEC. DNS lookups that must be private: DoH or DoT.
  5. Voice or video media: SRTP, with call setup protected by SIP over TLS.
  6. Check what the choice leaves exposed, such as the subject line or which names are looked up.

Hands-on lab

See DNSSEC signatures and a failed validation

About 30 min. dig in your lab VM

  1. In your lab VM, run: dig ietf.org +dnssec. Find the RRSIG record in the answer section and note the algorithm number and the signer's name.
  2. Run: dig ietf.org DNSKEY +short and note that the zone publishes its public keys in DNS.
  3. Run: dig @1.1.1.1 ietf.org +dnssec and look for the ad flag in the header. It means the resolver validated the signatures.
  4. Run: dig @1.1.1.1 dnssec-failed.org, a test domain that is deliberately signed incorrectly. Note the SERVFAIL status. Then run the same query with +cd, which asks the resolver to skip checking, and compare.

Expected: You see RRSIG and DNSKEY records for a signed zone, the ad flag from a validating resolver, and SERVFAIL for the broken test domain until checking is disabled.

The signatures are public, like the records themselves, so DNSSEC hides nothing. Its value shows in the last step: a validating resolver refuses an answer it cannot verify, instead of passing a possibly forged address to the user.

Done when:

  • You identified an RRSIG record and its signer.
  • You explained why dnssec-failed.org returns SERVFAIL, and what +cd changed.

3. See it in real products

Signatures only protect people if the interface makes them meaningful. Mail and messaging products decide whether a valid signature is shown at all, how a broken or missing one is shown, and whether a lookalike sender with a valid signature from the wrong organization looks trustworthy. Badges that appear on everything teach people to ignore them.

In security operations

  • A finance team requires that payment instructions arrive signed with S/MIME. A forged request from a lookalike address has no valid signature, so the mail client shows no trusted signature mark, and the team does not act on it.
  • A resolver that validates DNSSEC receives an answer for a bank's domain whose signature does not match the published key. It returns an error instead of the forged address, so the user never reaches the attacker's server.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design a trustworthy signed-message indicator

About 45 min

Scenario: A business email product is adding S/MIME support for finance and legal teams. Customers want staff to notice when a payment instruction is not signed by the real sender, without every message gaining a badge that people stop reading.

Problem statement: Today the product shows a small padlock for any message that arrived over TLS, which users read as "this sender is genuine", including on forged messages from lookalike domains.

Your design task:

  • Define each signature state, from valid by a trusted colleague to invalid or missing, and decide what is shown for each.
  • Design how the sender's verified identity is shown next to the claimed display name, including the case where they differ.
  • Design the interruption when a message that policy says must be signed arrives unsigned or with a broken signature.
  • Remove or rename the misleading transport padlock.

Technical security constraints:

  • Positive indicators must be rare and specific, so they keep their meaning.
  • Every state must be understandable without colour alone and announced by screen readers.
  • Users cannot be asked to inspect certificates to make a decision.

User needs:

  • Finance staff need to know, at the moment of acting, whether a payment request is genuinely from the named colleague.
  • Administrators need to set which senders or message types require a valid signature.

Expected deliverable: A signature state table, the message header design for each state, and the interruption flow for a required signature that fails.

Evaluation criteria:

  • States are distinguishable without colour, and no state requires reading a certificate.
  • A lookalike sender with a valid signature from another organization is not shown as trusted.
  • The indicator appears only where it changes a decision, so it is not ignored.
  • The design retires the misleading padlock and explains the change to existing users.

Senior-level considerations:

  • Start from the decisions people make, such as paying an invoice, and place the signal there rather than on every message.
  • Treat a missing signature on a message that should be signed as more alarming than an ordinary unsigned message.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Which signature checks do we run, and what states can a result be in: valid, invalid, expired, untrusted issuer, or no signature?

Security:

  • Which messages should require a valid signature, such as payment changes, and what happens when one fails?

Product:

  • Where do people make decisions based on who sent a message, and what do they look at today?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: An organization wants three things: lawyers must be able to prove that a contract email came from them and was not altered, staff on hotel Wi-Fi should not reveal which websites they look up, and internal calls must not be readable by anyone capturing network traffic. Which protocol meets each need?

Compare your reasoning

S/MIME signing for the contract emails, since it gives integrity and non-repudiation that stay with the message. DNS over HTTPS or DNS over TLS for private lookups, since DNSSEC proves answers but does not hide them. SRTP for the call media, with SIP over TLS protecting call setup.

1. A user must prove that an email came from them and was not changed, even after it is forwarded. What should they use?

  • An S/MIME digital signature
  • TLS between mail servers
  • An SPF record
  • DNS over HTTPS
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: An S/MIME digital signature. The signature travels with the message and can be checked by anyone with the sender's certificate.
  • Incorrect: TLS between mail servers. TLS protects the message only while it moves between two servers.
  • Incorrect: An SPF record. SPF tells servers which systems may send for a domain. It does not prove who wrote a message.
  • Incorrect: DNS over HTTPS. This protects DNS lookups, not email.

2. What does DNSSEC provide?

  • Integrity and proof of origin for DNS answers
  • Encryption of DNS queries
  • Encryption of web traffic
  • Authentication of email senders
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Integrity and proof of origin for DNS answers. Resolvers verify signatures, so forged or altered answers are rejected.
  • Incorrect: Encryption of DNS queries. DNSSEC does not encrypt anything. DoH and DoT do that.
  • Incorrect: Encryption of web traffic. That is the role of TLS.
  • Incorrect: Authentication of email senders. That is the role of S/MIME, and of SPF, DKIM and DMARC at the domain level.

3. Which protocol protects the audio of a voice call from being read by someone capturing traffic?

  • SRTP
  • SIP over TLS
  • DNSSEC
  • S/MIME
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: SRTP. It encrypts and authenticates the media packets.
  • Incorrect: SIP over TLS. This protects call setup, not the audio itself.
  • Incorrect: DNSSEC. This protects DNS answers.
  • Incorrect: S/MIME. This protects email.

4. True or false: turning on DNSSEC stops a network observer from seeing which websites a user looks up.

  • False
  • True
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: False. DNSSEC signs answers but does not encrypt them. Encrypted DNS such as DoH or DoT hides lookups.
  • Incorrect: True. Signing does not hide the content.

5. Match each need to the protocol that meets it.

Correct matches
  • Prove a DNS answer is genuine: DNSSEC
  • Keep DNS lookups private on public Wi-Fi: DNS over HTTPS
  • Encrypt and sign one email end to end: S/MIME
  • Encrypt voice and video media: SRTP

Decide first what is protected, then whether the need is privacy or authenticity.

6. A validating resolver checks a zone's DNSKEY against a record published by the parent zone. Which record is that?

  • DS
  • RRSIG
  • MX
  • TXT
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: DS. The delegation signer record links the parent's trust to the child's key.
  • Incorrect: RRSIG. This holds the signature over a set of records in the zone itself.
  • Incorrect: MX. This names mail servers.
  • Incorrect: TXT. This holds free text, such as SPF policies.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. Which design best helps staff spot a forged payment request?

  • Show the verified signer's identity beside the display name, and interrupt when a payment request that must be signed is not
  • Show a padlock on every message that arrived encrypted in transit
  • Put a green tick on all internal messages
  • Add a link to view the raw certificate details
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Show the verified signer's identity beside the display name, and interrupt when a payment request that must be signed is not. The signal appears where the decision is made, and absence is treated as a warning.
  • Incorrect: Show a padlock on every message that arrived encrypted in transit. Transport encryption says nothing about who wrote the message.
  • Incorrect: Put a green tick on all internal messages. Constant positive badges stop carrying meaning.
  • Incorrect: Add a link to view the raw certificate details. Users should not need to inspect certificates to decide.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Thinking DNSSEC encrypts DNS. It signs answers. Privacy needs DNS over HTTPS or DNS over TLS.
  • Treating SPF, DKIM and DMARC as email encryption. They are domain checks done by servers; S/MIME protects the message itself.
  • Assuming SRTP protects the whole call. Call setup with SIP needs its own protection, such as TLS.

Summary

  • S/MIME seals one email: signature for proof, encryption for privacy, with certificates on both sides.
  • DNSSEC proves DNS answers are genuine; DoH and DoT keep lookups private. They solve different problems.
  • SRTP protects call media; SIP over TLS protects call setup.

Flashcards

Does DNSSEC encrypt DNS queries?

No. It signs answers so they can be verified. DoH and DoT encrypt queries.

Which protocol encrypts voice and video media?

SRTP. Call setup is protected separately, for example with SIP over TLS.

What does an S/MIME signature give that SPF, DKIM and DMARC do not?

Proof of the individual sender and integrity that stays with the message, end to end.
