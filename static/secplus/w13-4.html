Security compliance and privacy

Lesson 54 · Week 13 · Domain 5: Security Program Management and Oversight · Objective 5.4 · Intermediate · about 100 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Describe compliance training and monitoring.
  • List consequences of non-compliance.
  • Explain privacy rights and legal holds.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Compliance failures bring fines, lost licenses and lost trust. Privacy obligations affect how systems must be built, including what they can delete.

In plain language

Compliance is like following the rules of the road. You learn them, there are checks and there are penalties. Privacy law gives people rights over information about them, such as asking for corrections.

2. Technical deep dive

Compliance training covers data handling, anti-money laundering/counter-terrorism financing (AML/CTF) and anti-bribery. Compliance monitoring uses attestations (formal statements that requirements are met) and acknowledgements (recording that someone has read and accepted something). Consequences of non-compliance include reputational damage, financial penalties, legal action, contractual breach, sanctions and loss of license.

Privacy concepts include the right to be forgotten (erasure), opt-in or opt-out consent choices, data correction, processing restrictions, processing prevention, the difference between controller vs. processor, and ownership of data. GDPR gives broad rights to individuals in the EU. In Canada, PIPEDA governs private-sector personal information in commercial activity federally, and some provinces, such as British Columbia, Alberta and Quebec, have their own substantially similar laws. FINTRAC is Canada's AML regulator.

Legal compliance covers a legal hold, which suspends normal deletion when litigation is reasonably expected; legal orders such as subpoenas or warrants; and data retention requirements, which set minimum or maximum retention periods.

Walkthrough: Handle a deletion request

  1. Verify the person's identity.
  2. Find where their data is stored, including backups and processors.
  3. Check for legal holds or retention requirements.
  4. Delete what can be deleted, and record the action.
  5. Respond within the required time.

3. See it in real products

Privacy is directly experienced in products: consent prompts, cookie banners, settings, notices and retention choices. Dark patterns can turn consent into coercion. Good privacy design gives people real choices and respects the difference between controllers and processors and between regional obligations.

In security operations

  • A company receiving a deletion request must also check whether a legal hold requires keeping that data.
  • A Canadian retailer collecting customer data must follow PIPEDA or a substantially similar provincial law.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design privacy controls and consent

About 45 min

Scenario: A health and wellness app wants to use member data to personalize content and share aggregated insights with partners. It operates in Canada and the EU.

Problem statement: Currently one checkbox at sign-up covers everything, and members can't later change their choices or see what is being shared.

Your design task:

  • Separate necessary processing from optional uses.
  • Design a layered consent moment.
  • Design a privacy center: choices, history, access, deletion.
  • Show what changes when consent is withdrawn.

Technical security constraints:

  • Withdrawing consent must be as easy as giving it, and must really stop the processing.
  • Health data has stricter handling obligations in many jurisdictions.
  • Regional legal requirements must be confirmed by privacy counsel.

User needs:

  • Members need to understand trade-offs in plain language.
  • The business needs valid, recorded consent.
  • Privacy officers need proof of what was agreed and when.

Expected deliverable: A layered consent flow, a privacy center and a data-use map.

Evaluation criteria:

  • Consent is granular, withdrawable and technically enforced.
  • The design avoids dark patterns and considers re-identification from aggregated data.
  • Choices are written in plain language at the point of decision.
  • The design makes clear which assumptions need legal confirmation.

Senior-level considerations:

  • Privacy is more than compliance, so ask whether a person would be surprised by this data use.
  • Legal review confirms requirements, and your job is making the choice real for users.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Can consent be enforced technically, so data use actually stops?

Security:

  • Which data flows change depending on region?

Product:

  • Which privacy regulations apply, and who confirms that?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A customer asks to have their data deleted, but the company has been told to preserve records relating to a lawsuit that includes that customer. What takes priority?

Compare your reasoning

The legal hold. Relevant data must be preserved. The company should explain the limitation, and handle the deletion when the hold ends, under legal guidance.

1. What does a legal hold do?

  • Suspends normal deletion of relevant data
  • Encrypts backups
  • Removes user accounts
  • Transfers risk
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Suspends normal deletion of relevant data. It preserves potential evidence.
  • Incorrect: Encrypts backups. It is not an encryption mechanism.
  • Incorrect: Removes user accounts. It does not.
  • Incorrect: Transfers risk. It does not.

2. A person asks an organization to delete their personal data. Which right is this?

  • Right to be forgotten
  • Data correction
  • Opt-in
  • Attestation
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Right to be forgotten. It concerns erasure.
  • Incorrect: Data correction. This is about fixing data.
  • Incorrect: Opt-in. This is about consent.
  • Incorrect: Attestation. This is a compliance statement.

3. Employees sign a form stating that they read the policy. What is this?

  • An acknowledgement
  • A sanction
  • A legal order
  • A CVSS score
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: An acknowledgement. It records acceptance.
  • Incorrect: A sanction. This is a penalty.
  • Incorrect: A legal order. This comes from a court or authority.
  • Incorrect: A CVSS score. This relates to vulnerabilities.

4. Which are consequences of non-compliance? Choose two.

  • Sanctions
  • Loss of license
  • Faster network speeds
  • Automatic patching
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Sanctions. Authorities may impose penalties.
  • Correct: Loss of license. An organization can lose the right to operate.
  • Incorrect: Faster network speeds. This is not a consequence.
  • Incorrect: Automatic patching. This is not a consequence.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. Which pattern best supports meaningful consent?

  • Separate, unticked choices and an equally easy decline
  • A single pre-ticked box that covers every possible data use
  • A large Accept button beside a tiny hidden decline link
  • Consent wording placed only inside the full terms of service
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Separate, unticked choices and an equally easy decline. People make specific choices without being steered.
  • Incorrect: A single pre-ticked box that covers every possible data use. Pre-ticked, bundled consent doesn't reflect a real choice.
  • Incorrect: A large Accept button beside a tiny hidden decline link. This steers people, which makes it a dark pattern.
  • Incorrect: Consent wording placed only inside the full terms of service. People can't make a specific choice from buried terms.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Confusing a legal hold with data retention rules.
  • Assuming deletion is always required and always possible.

Summary

  • Training and monitoring support compliance.
  • Non-compliance has many kinds of consequence.
  • Privacy rights must be balanced with legal duties.
