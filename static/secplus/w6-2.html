Threat actors and what motivates them

Lesson 23 · Week 6 · Domain 2: Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks · Objective 2.2 · Beginner · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Professional relevance for cross-functional work

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Identify each type of threat actor.
  • Match actors to likely motivations.
  • Describe actors using their attributes.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

The defenses that stop a bored teenager differ from those that slow a national intelligence service. Knowing who is likely to target you and why helps decide where to invest.

In plain language

Burglars are not all alike. A teenager rattling door handles, a professional crew with a plan and a disgruntled former employee who still has a key are very different problems. Security uses the same thinking.

2. Technical deep dive

Threat actors are described by attributes. Internal or external describes where they start from. Resources and funding range from none to national budgets. The level of sophistication and capability ranges from running other people's tools to developing custom exploits.

Motivations include financial gain; influence over opinion or events; theft of intellectual property; notoriety or personal reputation; espionage; causing fear or chaos; extortion; general curiosity; revenge; ideological belief; political goals; and ethical reasons, such as a researcher who reports a flaw or believes they are exposing wrongdoing. Someone may act ethically, and still act without authorization, which remains illegal.

Walkthrough: Profile an actor from clues

  1. Clue: custom tools, activity over many months, focus on defense research.
  2. Resources are high, and sophistication is high.
  3. The motive appears to be espionage, because nothing was sold or disrupted.
  4. The most consistent actor is state-sponsored.
  5. Note that attribution is hard and should be expressed with caution.

Hands-on lab

Build a threat profile

About 35 min. MITRE ATT&CK Groups pages in a browser

  1. Open MITRE ATT&CK and go to the Groups section. Choose one group.
  2. Record the group's reported sponsor or motivation, its targets and three techniques it has used.
  3. Classify it using the actor types and three attributes in this lesson.
  4. Name two controls in your own imagined organization that would address those techniques.

Expected: A short profile that links an actor to attributes and defenses.

This is how threat-informed defense starts: choose controls based on how likely attackers really behave.

Done when:

  • Your profile states actor type, motivation and three attributes.
  • Your two controls relate to the techniques you listed.

3. See it in real products

Threat actors are the opposite of personas: people with goals your product should frustrate. An abuse case asks how a disgruntled employee, a fraudster or an automated bot would use a feature, which often exposes gaps that happy-path design misses.

In security operations

  • A ransomware group sells access, handles negotiation and offers customer support. That business-like structure is a mark of organized crime.
  • An employee emails a client list to the wrong person. There was no malice, but it is still an insider incident: an accidental one.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

No design exercise here, on purpose. Abuse thinking is applied in every exercise in the Week 7 threat lessons.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Which of our features would be most valuable to abuse?

Security:

  • Which types of attacker are realistic for our product and customers?

Product:

  • Which abuse has already happened, and what did it cost?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A former employee's account deletes project files on the day after their dismissal. The attacker used a valid password. Which actor type and motive fit best, and what process failed?

Compare your reasoning

An insider, or former insider, motivated by revenge. Offboarding failed, because the account should have been disabled at departure.

1. Which actor is usually most motivated by money?

  • Organized crime
  • Hacktivist
  • Unskilled attacker
  • Accidental insider
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Organized crime. Profit is the defining goal.
  • Incorrect: Hacktivist. Hacktivists are motivated by a cause.
  • Incorrect: Unskilled attacker. These are often seeking attention or curiosity.
  • Incorrect: Accidental insider. There is no intent.

2. An attacker uses custom zero-day exploits and stays hidden for many months to steal defense documents. What is the most likely actor?

  • State-sponsored
  • Unskilled attacker
  • Accidental insider
  • Hacktivist
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: State-sponsored. Very high resources and a focus on espionage fit.
  • Incorrect: Unskilled attacker. Custom zero-days require far more capability.
  • Incorrect: Accidental insider. This is deliberate activity.
  • Incorrect: Hacktivist. Long, secret theft of defense documents does not fit a public cause.

3. Match each scenario to its likely motivation.

Correct matches
  • Encrypts files and demands payment: Extortion
  • Defaces a site to protest a law: Ideological
  • Steals a rival's product designs: Intellectual property
  • Fired employee destroys records: Revenge

Look for what the attacker wanted to achieve.

4. An employee sends a spreadsheet of customer data to the wrong person. What is this?

  • An unintentional insider incident
  • A state-sponsored attack
  • A hacktivist campaign
  • A supply-chain attack
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: An unintentional insider incident. Harm occurred without malicious intent.
  • Incorrect: A state-sponsored attack. Nothing suggests an external government.
  • Incorrect: A hacktivist campaign. There is no cause involved.
  • Incorrect: A supply-chain attack. No third party was involved.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Assuming insiders are always malicious. Many incidents are honest mistakes.
  • Believing only large companies are targets. Automated attacks target anyone vulnerable.

Summary

  • Actors differ in location, resources and sophistication.
  • Motivation predicts what they will target.
  • Use attributes to select appropriate defenses.
