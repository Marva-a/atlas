The core ideas: CIA, AAA, non-repudiation, Zero Trust and defense in depth

Lesson 17 · Week 5 · Domain 1: General Security Concepts · Objective 1.1 · Beginner · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Apply confidentiality, integrity and availability to a real situation.
  • Separate authentication, authorization and accounting, and explain non-repudiation.
  • Describe defense in depth, least privilege and Zero Trust principles.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

These ideas are the vocabulary of the entire field. Every control you will study protects confidentiality, integrity or availability, and many exam scenarios simply ask which of those was harmed.

In plain language

Think of your medical records. Confidentiality means strangers cannot read them. Integrity means nobody can quietly change your blood type. Availability means the doctor can open them when you are in the emergency room.

AAA is airport security. Authentication checks your passport, authorization checks that your ticket lets you board this flight, and accounting records that you boarded. Non-repudiation is a signed receipt: you cannot later claim it was not you.

Zero Trust is like a hospital where your badge is checked at every door, not only at the entrance. Being inside the building earns no automatic trust.

2. Technical deep dive

The CIA triad is confidentiality, integrity and availability. Encryption and access control protect confidentiality. Hashes and digital signatures detect changes, which supports integrity. Redundancy and backups support availability.

AAA stands for authentication, authorization and accounting. Authentication proves identity. Authorization grants permissions. Accounting records what was done. Non-repudiation means someone cannot credibly deny having performed an action, and is commonly achieved through digital signatures tied to a private key that only that person controls.

Defense in depth layers different controls so that one failure does not lead to total compromise. Least privilege gives each user or process only the access needed, for only as long as needed. NIST SP 800-207 describes Zero Trust as moving defenses away from network location and toward users, assets and resources. Principles include verifying each request, assuming breach, granting per-session least privilege and using device and context signals in decisions.

In the Zero Trust architecture, a Policy Decision Point (PDP) decides whether to allow access, and a Policy Enforcement Point (PEP) carries out that decision between the subject and the resource. These terms come from NIST SP 800-207 and are useful even though the current exam list focuses on the principles.

Authentication proves who. Authorization decides what. Accounting records what happened.

Defense in depth. If one layer fails, others still stand between an attacker and the data.

The decision point decides, using identity, device and context. The enforcement point stands in the path and carries the decision out, one request at a time.

Key terms

ConfidentialityKeeping information from people who should not see it.
IntegrityMaking sure data is not changed without authorization, or that changes are detected.
AvailabilityMaking sure systems and data can be used when needed.
Non-repudiationAssurance that someone cannot deny having performed an action.
Defense in depthUsing multiple layers of different controls.
Least privilegeGiving only the minimum access needed to do a task.
Zero Trust principlesNever granting trust from location alone: verify each request, limit access and assume breach.
Policy Decision PointThe Zero Trust component that decides whether to allow access.
Policy Enforcement PointThe Zero Trust component that enforces that decision.

Walkthrough: Identify which part of CIA was harmed

  1. Ask: did someone see what they should not? That is confidentiality.
  2. Ask: was something altered, added or deleted without permission? That is integrity.
  3. Ask: could people not use it when they needed it? That is availability.
  4. Remember that a single incident can harm more than one.
  5. Choose the control that addresses the harm you identified.

Hands-on lab

Build a CIA and control map

About 35 min. A table in any editor

  1. Choose a service you use, such as online banking.
  2. List two things that would harm each of confidentiality, integrity and availability.
  3. Add one control for each harm, and label which layer of defense in depth it belongs to.
  4. Add one example of accounting, and state what would provide non-repudiation.

Expected: A six-row table of harms with matching controls across more than one layer.

Mapping harm to control is the core skill. If all your controls sit in one layer, you have found a gap.

Done when:

  • Every CIA element has two harms and controls.
  • Your controls cover at least three layers.

3. See it in real products

Confidentiality, integrity and availability often pull against one another in an interface. A payment approval that needs a second verification protects integrity and costs a little speed. An audit entry that records who changed a limit supports non-repudiation. Zero Trust shows up as being asked to verify again when an action is risky, even though you are already signed in.

In security operations

  • Ransomware encrypting a hospital's files harms availability. If the attackers also leak the files, confidentiality is harmed too.
  • A company requires a badge at the door, a firewall at the network edge, MFA at sign-in and encryption on disks. Each layer covers failure in another. That is defense in depth.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design step-up verification for a high-risk change

About 60 min

Scenario: A business banking app lets finance administrators change the bank account used for supplier payments. Attackers who take over a session target this action first, because a single change redirects later payments.

Problem statement: Today the change takes one click once someone is signed in. You must protect it without making routine finance work painful.

Your design task:

  • Decide which CIA and AAA goals this flow is protecting, in writing.
  • Design the flow for changing payee details, including step-up verification and a second approver.
  • Design the confirmation shown to the approver, and the record that appears in the audit trail.
  • Design the failure states for a declined verification and a timed-out approval.

Technical security constraints:

  • An existing session alone must not authorize the change.
  • The person who requests a change cannot be the one who approves it.
  • Approvers must see the real old and new values as held by the server.

User needs:

  • Finance administrators need to complete legitimate changes on deadline.
  • Approvers need enough context to spot a fraudulent change.
  • Auditors need to know who did what and when.

Expected deliverable: A user flow with key screens, a failure-state list and a short written rationale that names the CIA and AAA goals.

Evaluation criteria:

  • The flow enforces step-up verification and separation of duties.
  • The design addresses a compromised session and a pressured or careless approver.
  • Approvers can compare old and new values at a glance.
  • The rationale states the trade-off accepted and how its cost would be measured.

Senior-level considerations:

  • Reserve friction for rare, high-consequence moments, because constant prompts train people to approve without reading.
  • Bring security in to decide which verification method is strong enough, since that is not a purely visual decision.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Can we require a fresh verification for a specific action without ending the session?
  • Are audit entries written by the server, and can anyone edit them?

Security:

  • Which actions in this product count as high risk?

Product:

  • How much friction will customers accept on this action, and how will we measure it?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A hospital's patient records were altered by an unknown party, but no record was viewed by outsiders and the system stayed online. Which CIA element was affected, and which control best detects this?

Compare your reasoning

Integrity was affected, since data was changed without authorization. File integrity monitoring or cryptographic hashes would detect the change. Confidentiality was not harmed in this description, and availability was not affected.

1. A denial-of-service attack takes a shopping site offline. Which element is directly harmed?

  • Availability
  • Confidentiality
  • Integrity
  • Non-repudiation
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Availability. Users cannot use the service when they need it.
  • Incorrect: Confidentiality. No information was disclosed in this scenario.
  • Incorrect: Integrity. No data was changed in this scenario.
  • Incorrect: Non-repudiation. This concerns denying actions, which is not involved here.

2. A company needs to prove that a specific manager approved a contract, in a way the manager cannot convincingly deny. What provides this?

  • A digital signature created with the manager's private key
  • A shared team password
  • Full disk encryption
  • A symmetric key known to both parties
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: A digital signature created with the manager's private key. Only the holder of the private key can create it, which supports non-repudiation.
  • Incorrect: A shared team password. A shared credential cannot tie the action to one person.
  • Incorrect: Full disk encryption. This protects stored data but does not prove who acted.
  • Incorrect: A symmetric key known to both parties. Either party could have produced the result, so it cannot prove which did.

3. Which activity is accounting?

  • Recording which files a user accessed and when
  • Checking a user's password
  • Granting a user access to a folder
  • Encrypting a user's laptop
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Recording which files a user accessed and when. Accounting tracks activity.
  • Incorrect: Checking a user's password. That is authentication.
  • Incorrect: Granting a user access to a folder. That is authorization.
  • Incorrect: Encrypting a user's laptop. That is a data protection control.

4. An employee is on the office network and has already signed in. Under Zero Trust, what should happen when the employee requests a sensitive application?

  • The request is evaluated on identity, device condition and context before access
  • Access is granted because the employee is on the internal network
  • Access is granted because the employee signed in earlier today
  • The firewall is turned off for trusted users
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: The request is evaluated on identity, device condition and context before access. Zero Trust grants no trust based on network location.
  • Incorrect: Access is granted because the employee is on the internal network. Location alone must not grant trust.
  • Incorrect: Access is granted because the employee signed in earlier today. Access decisions are made per session and per resource.
  • Incorrect: The firewall is turned off for trusted users. This removes a layer of defense.

5. Which arrangements show defense in depth? Choose two.

  • Firewall at the edge, plus EDR on endpoints
  • MFA at sign-in, plus least-privilege permissions
  • Two identical firewalls with the same rule set, as the only control
  • One strong password on the administrator account
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Firewall at the edge, plus EDR on endpoints. Different layers protect against different failures.
  • Correct: MFA at sign-in, plus least-privilege permissions. Each limits the damage if the other fails.
  • Incorrect: Two identical firewalls with the same rule set, as the only control. Duplication of one control type gives redundancy, not layered defense.
  • Incorrect: One strong password on the administrator account. This is a single control.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. An approval screen shows only "Approve change to payee?" with an Approve button. What is the most serious flaw?

  • The approver cannot see what is actually being changed
  • The button is not large enough for quick use
  • The wording uses the word payee instead of supplier
  • The screen does not offer a dark mode preference
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: The approver cannot see what is actually being changed. Without the values, approval gives no real protection against fraud.
  • Incorrect: The button is not large enough for quick use. Speed is not what this control exists to provide.
  • Incorrect: The wording uses the word payee instead of supplier. Terminology matters, but it is minor compared with the missing facts.
  • Incorrect: The screen does not offer a dark mode preference. Appearance options do not affect the integrity of this decision.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Confusing integrity with non-repudiation. Integrity is about whether data changed. Non-repudiation is about whether someone can deny an action.
  • Equating Zero Trust with a product. It is a set of principles and an architecture.

Summary

  • CIA describes what security protects.
  • AAA proves who you are, sets what you may do and records what you did.
  • Defense in depth and least privilege limit the damage from any single failure.
