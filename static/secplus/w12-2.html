Incident response

Lesson 48 · Week 12 · Domain 4: Security Operations · Objective 4.7 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Follow the incident response stages in order.
  • Handle evidence with chain of custody.
  • Decide who must be notified.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Every organization will have incidents. A rehearsed response limits damage, and mistakes made in panic can destroy evidence or make things worse.

In plain language

Responding to an incident is like responding to a fire. You prepare with drills and extinguishers, then detect the fire, work out what is burning, contain it, put it out, repair the damage and review what happened so it does not recur.

2. Technical deep dive

Preparation includes training, testing, tabletop exercises (discussion-based walkthroughs), playbooks, simulation (practice using realistic scenarios) and assigning roles. Identification includes detection through alerts, internal advisory and external advisory sources and proactive threat hunting, which searches for attackers who have evaded detection.

Investigation includes digital forensics, chain of custody (documenting who handled evidence and when), e-discovery (identifying and producing electronic information for legal purposes) and preservation of evidence. Containment limits spread, for example quarantine/isolation of a host. Negotiation may involve a specialist negotiator in extortion events, working with legal counsel and considering sanctions and law enforcement guidance.

Eradication/recovery removes the cause and restores service. Notification/external reporting covers stakeholders, customers, law enforcement and mandatory reporting to regulators, sometimes within strict time limits. Post-incident work includes lessons learned, root cause analysis and post-incident reporting (PIR).

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 now aligns incident response with the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 functions instead of a separate four-phase cycle. Follow the stage names in CompTIA's objectives for the exam, and know that real organizations may use other models.

The stages overlap in practice, and lessons learned feed back into preparation.

Walkthrough: First hour of a ransomware alert

  1. Confirm the alert and declare an incident according to the plan.
  2. Assemble the response team and open a timeline.
  3. Isolate affected hosts while preserving evidence.
  4. Identify the scope and the initial vector.
  5. Begin notification decisions with legal and leadership.

3. See it in real products

For users, an incident is a personal event: their account was used, their money moved, their trust has gone. The stages of incident response map to their journey: detection becomes a warning, containment locks the attacker out, eradication removes the attacker's access, recovery restores the person, and lessons learned change the product.

In security operations

  • A tabletop exercise shows that nobody knows who can authorize taking the payment system offline. That is cheaper to learn before an incident.
  • An investigator seals a laptop, records the time and signs a chain of custody form when handing it to the forensics team.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design account compromise investigation and recovery

About 60 min

Scenario: Attackers take over accounts in a personal finance app through reused passwords. They add new payees, change the notification email and create forwarding rules.

Problem statement: Support has a set of manual steps, and victims get no clear guidance. Sessions stay active and attacker changes remain after a password reset.

Your design task:

  • Map incident stages to the user journey and to the support console.
  • Design the "This wasn't me" entry point and secure lockdown.
  • Design the review of what changed: payees, email, devices, rules.
  • Design recovery and the follow-up communications.

Technical security constraints:

  • A password reset alone doesn't end existing sessions or undo changes.
  • The attacker may control the email address on file, so recovery can't rely only on it.
  • Evidence must be preserved before accounts are altered.

User needs:

  • Victims are stressed, so they need one clear next step at a time.
  • Support staff need a complete picture of the attacker's actions.
  • Security staff need preserved evidence and patterns.

Expected deliverable: An incident journey map and designs for lockdown, change review and recovery.

Evaluation criteria:

  • The design ends sessions and reverses attacker changes, and it preserves evidence.
  • The design considers an attacker who still controls the email or tries to use the recovery path.
  • Language is calm, specific and doesn't blame the user.
  • Lessons learned feed back into product changes, with owners.

Senior-level considerations:

  • Avoid telling users that everything is safe until the investigation supports that statement.
  • Legal and communications must approve notifications, and the design should allow for that time.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Which sessions, tokens and devices can we revoke for one account?
  • What changes did the attacker make that we must detect and reverse?

Security:

  • What must we preserve as evidence before changing anything?

Product:

  • Who communicates with affected users, and what are our legal notification duties?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: An analyst finds malware on a laptop and immediately wipes and reinstalls it. Which incident response problems does this cause?

Compare your reasoning

It destroys evidence before investigation, so scope and root cause may never be known and any legal use is lost. The host should have been isolated and evidence preserved first.

1. Put these stages in the order given in the exam objectives.

Correct order
  1. Preparation
  2. Identification
  3. Investigation
  4. Containment
  5. Eradication/recovery
  6. Post-incident

Each stage relies on knowledge gained in earlier stages, and post-incident work feeds preparation.

2. What does chain of custody document?

  • Who handled evidence and when
  • Which firewall rules were applied
  • Which certificates were revoked
  • Which staff took training
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Who handled evidence and when. This helps show it was not tampered with.
  • Incorrect: Which firewall rules were applied. This is configuration documentation.
  • Incorrect: Which certificates were revoked. This is PKI.
  • Incorrect: Which staff took training. This is a training record.

3. A compromised host is spreading malware. What is the priority action?

  • Isolate the host from the network
  • Wipe it immediately
  • Ignore it until the weekly meeting
  • Post the incident on social media
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Isolate the host from the network. This limits spread while preserving evidence.
  • Incorrect: Wipe it immediately. This destroys evidence.
  • Incorrect: Ignore it until the weekly meeting. This allows further spread.
  • Incorrect: Post the incident on social media. This is not appropriate.

4. Analysts proactively search for signs of an attacker that alerts have not flagged. What is this?

  • Threat hunting
  • Tabletop exercise
  • Fail forward
  • Tokenization
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Threat hunting. It is proactive and hypothesis-driven.
  • Incorrect: Tabletop exercise. This is discussion-based practice.
  • Incorrect: Fail forward. This is a change approach.
  • Incorrect: Tokenization. This is a data method.

5. A breach affects personal data covered by a regulation with a notification deadline. Who should drive that decision?

  • Legal and compliance staff, with leadership, following the incident plan
  • The most junior analyst alone
  • The attacker
  • Nobody, if the data is backed up
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Legal and compliance staff, with leadership, following the incident plan. Legal obligations need qualified judgment.
  • Incorrect: The most junior analyst alone. This is not appropriate.
  • Incorrect: The attacker. This is absurd.
  • Incorrect: Nobody, if the data is backed up. Backups do not remove notification duties.

6. When should a lessons learned review take place?

  • After the incident has been resolved
  • Only before an incident begins
  • Only if a regulator demands it
  • Never, so as to keep incidents quiet
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: After the incident has been resolved. It belongs in post-incident activity.
  • Incorrect: Only before an incident begins. Nothing has occurred yet.
  • Incorrect: Only if a regulator demands it. It should be routine.
  • Incorrect: Never, so as to keep incidents quiet. This wastes the chance to improve.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. A user reports a takeover and resets their password. The attacker's device is still signed in. What was missed?

  • Revoking existing sessions and tokens during containment
  • Asking the user to choose a much longer password
  • Sending a satisfaction survey after the case is closed
  • Deleting all the logs so that the account starts clean
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Revoking existing sessions and tokens during containment. A password reset alone often leaves existing sessions active.
  • Incorrect: Asking the user to choose a much longer password. A stronger password doesn't end the attacker's active session.
  • Incorrect: Sending a satisfaction survey after the case is closed. A survey does nothing to contain the attacker.
  • Incorrect: Deleting all the logs so that the account starts clean. This destroys evidence and doesn't remove the attacker.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Wiping a machine before collecting evidence.
  • Skipping lessons learned once things are running again.

Summary

  • Prepare, identify, investigate, contain, eradicate and recover, then learn.
  • Preserve evidence and document handling.
  • Notification can be legally required.
