Threats, authentication, authorization and safe labs

Lesson 16 · Week 4 · Foundations · Foundation (not an exam objective) · Beginner · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Tell apart a threat, a vulnerability and a risk.
  • Separate authentication from authorization and name the factor types.
  • Follow the rules for safe, legal practice.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

These are the most frequently confused words in security. Getting them right now makes the exam domains easier. Safe lab habits matter too, because testing without permission can be illegal, however good the intent.

In plain language

A vulnerability is an unlocked window. A threat is a burglar in the area. Risk is the chance that a burglar comes through that window, combined with how much it would cost you. Authentication is showing your ID at the door. Authorization is the list that says which rooms you may enter.

2. Technical deep dive

A threat is anything that could cause harm. A vulnerability is a weakness that could be exploited. An exploit is the method that takes advantage of a vulnerability. Risk combines likelihood and impact. Authentication verifies identity, using something you know (a password), something you have (a phone or security key) or something you are (a fingerprint). Using two different factor types is multifactor authentication. Authorization then decides what an authenticated identity may do.

Lab safety rules: practise only on your own systems or on platforms that give explicit permission. Keep malware samples and attack tools in isolated VMs. Use snapshots. Never scan a network or a website without written authorization, even out of curiosity. In Canada and many other countries, unauthorized access to a computer is a crime.

Key terms

VulnerabilityA weakness that could be exploited.
ExploitA technique or code that takes advantage of a vulnerability.
RiskThe combination of how likely harm is and how bad it would be.

Walkthrough: Classify a security sentence

  1. Read: an old server runs software with a known flaw, and criminals are actively targeting that flaw.
  2. The old software is the vulnerability.
  3. The criminals are the threat.
  4. The chance and cost of them succeeding is the risk.
  5. Applying the patch removes the vulnerability and so reduces the risk.

Hands-on lab

Set your lab rules and test your VM's isolation

About 30 min. Your VM and a written checklist

  1. Write a one-page lab policy covering what you may test, where malware may be opened, and how you reset.
  2. Confirm your VM's network mode and that you have a recent snapshot.
  3. In the VM, ping your host computer's address and note the result.
  4. Enable MFA on one personal account of your own that offers it, and note the factors used.

Expected: A written policy and confirmed knowledge of how your VM connects to anything else.

Isolation only helps if you know exactly what the VM can reach. A written policy prevents one curious moment from becoming a legal or practical problem.

Done when:

  • Your written lab policy names what is and is not permitted.
  • You verified your VM's network mode and snapshot.

3. See it in real products

Authentication and authorization failures look similar to users and mean completely different things. "Please sign in" and "You don't have access to this project" lead to different next steps. Many products blur them, which sends people round in circles or reveals that a private record exists.

In security operations

  • Two passwords are not MFA, because both are something you know. A password and a code from an app is MFA.
  • A student scans a company's website out of curiosity and receives a legal complaint. Good intentions do not replace permission.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design the signed-out and no-access states

About 45 min

Scenario: Users of a project management product follow shared links to projects. Support reports confusion: people who are signed in as the wrong account, have lost access or were never invited all see the same "Something went wrong" page.

Problem statement: The product treats authentication failures, authorization failures and missing content as one error, so nobody knows what to do next.

Your design task:

  • List the distinct situations a person arriving from a shared link can be in.
  • Build a matrix that maps each situation to its message, primary action and what the page must not reveal.
  • Sketch the two most important states.

Technical security constraints:

  • A person with no access must not learn a private project's name or members.
  • A session can expire while a person is mid-task, so work in progress needs handling.
  • Different accounts may be signed in on the same browser.

User needs:

  • Invited users need the quickest route to the right account.
  • Blocked users need a legitimate way to ask for access.
  • Administrators need requests to reach the right person.

Expected deliverable: A state matrix plus two sketched screens.

Evaluation criteria:

  • The matrix treats authentication and authorization as separate conditions.
  • No state reveals the existence or details of a private project to someone without access.
  • Each state has one clear primary action written in plain language.
  • The request-access path considers who approves it and how abuse is limited.

Senior-level considerations:

  • A vague message can be the secure choice, so weigh usability against disclosure openly and record the decision.
  • Agree the wording of these states with engineering, because the response code and the message need to stay consistent.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Do we return the same response for a record that does not exist and one the user may not see?

Security:

  • Which of our error messages could confirm that a private item or an account exists?

Product:

  • Who should a blocked user contact, and can they request access inside the product?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A colleague signs in with the correct password and a phone code, but gets Access Denied when opening the finance folder. Did authentication or authorization fail?

Compare your reasoning

Authorization failed. The sign-in succeeded, so the system knows who the colleague is. The permissions for the finance folder then denied access.

1. An unpatched server is an example of which?

  • A vulnerability
  • A threat
  • A risk
  • A control
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: A vulnerability. It is a weakness that could be exploited.
  • Incorrect: A threat. A threat is what might exploit the weakness.
  • Incorrect: A risk. Risk also includes likelihood and impact.
  • Incorrect: A control. A control reduces risk.

2. Which combination is multifactor authentication?

  • A password and a code from a hardware token
  • A password and a PIN
  • Two different passwords
  • A username and a password
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: A password and a code from a hardware token. This combines something you know and something you have.
  • Incorrect: A password and a PIN. Both are something you know.
  • Incorrect: Two different passwords. Both are something you know.
  • Incorrect: A username and a password. A username is an identifier, and there is just one factor.

3. A user logs in successfully but cannot open a payroll report. What was denied?

  • Authorization
  • Authentication
  • Accounting
  • Encryption
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Authorization. The user's identity was confirmed, but the permission was missing.
  • Incorrect: Authentication. Authentication succeeded at login.
  • Incorrect: Accounting. Accounting records activity, and does not grant access.
  • Incorrect: Encryption. Encryption is not what controls this decision.

4. True or false: scanning a website you do not own is fine as long as you do not damage anything.

  • True
  • False
Answer and explanation
  • Incorrect: True. Testing without authorization can be illegal and against a provider's terms.
  • Correct: False. You need explicit permission, regardless of intent.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. A signed-in user opens a link to a project they were removed from. Which response fits best?

  • Say they lack access and offer a way to request it
  • Ask them to sign in again with the same account
  • Show the project name and owner so they recognise it
  • Show a server error page and suggest trying later
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Say they lack access and offer a way to request it. Identity is known, so this is an authorization outcome with a helpful next step.
  • Incorrect: Ask them to sign in again with the same account. Signing in again cannot change their permissions and wastes their time.
  • Incorrect: Show the project name and owner so they recognise it. Removed users should no longer receive details of the project.
  • Incorrect: Show a server error page and suggest trying later. This misdescribes the situation and leaves them with no route forward.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Using threat, vulnerability and risk as if they were the same word.
  • Believing that good intentions make unauthorized testing acceptable.

Summary

  • A vulnerability is a weakness, a threat could exploit it and risk combines likelihood and impact.
  • Authentication proves who, and authorization decides what.
  • Practise only where you have explicit permission.
