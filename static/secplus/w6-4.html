Threat vectors part 2: endpoints, supply chain, people, IoT, OT, physical and signals

Lesson 25 · Week 6 · Domain 2: Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks · Objective 2.3 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Professional relevance for cross-functional work

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Explain endpoint and living-off-the-land techniques.
  • Identify supply chain, human, external media, IoT, OT and physical vectors.
  • Describe signal-based vectors such as Bluetooth and NFC.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Attackers often go around strong perimeter defenses, through a supplier, a USB drive or a smart camera. These indirect routes are often less well watched.

In plain language

If the front door is strong, a burglar might arrive with the cleaning company, leave a free USB stick in the parking lot or use your own ladder from the garden shed. Those are the routes this lesson covers.

2. Technical deep dive

Endpoint-based vectors involve mobile devices, workstations, servers and tablets. Trusted devices can be abused because other systems accept them. Attackers also misuse built-in tools already on a system, called living-off-the-land tools, such as PowerShell or certutil, because activity using legitimate administration software is harder to spot.

Supply chain-based vectors exploit trust in third-party providers. These include managed service providers with remote access to many customers, logistic providers who handle physical goods, and software as a service (SaaS) providers holding customer data. A malicious software update is the classic example. External media-based vectors include a malicious Universal Serial Bus (USB) device, which may act as a keyboard or carry malware.

Human-based vectors include impersonation of a trusted person; a contractor or a visitor given more access than needed; a biometric weakness, such as spoofing a fingerprint or face; and a watering hole attack, which compromises a website the target group visits regularly.

Internet of Things (IoT) based vectors include cameras, sensors and printers, which often have weak defaults and rarely receive updates. Operational technology (OT) based vectors affect industrial control systems, where a compromise can have physical consequences and patching is difficult. Physical-based vectors include defeating lock and key systems, bypassing an access vestibule and stealing or cloning access passes. An access vestibule, often called a mantrap, is a small space with two doors that do not open at once.

Signal-based vectors use wireless channels. Bluetooth can be abused to pair without consent or to send unsolicited messages. Radio frequency (RF) attacks include jamming and interception. Near-field communications (NFC) operate at very short range, for tap-to-pay and access cards, and can be subject to relay and cloning.

Walkthrough: Trace a supply chain attack

  1. An attacker compromises a software vendor's build system.
  2. Malicious code is added to a signed update.
  3. Customers trust and install the update automatically.
  4. The attacker gains access inside each customer.
  5. Defenses include vendor assessment, monitoring and limiting what the software can reach.

Hands-on lab

Map your own vectors

About 35 min. A table in any editor

  1. List the devices, suppliers and services you rely on, such as a smart TV, a router, an email provider.
  2. For each item, name the vector category it falls under.
  3. For the three riskiest, choose one mitigation.
  4. On a Windows machine, note which built-in tools (such as PowerShell) a defender would want to watch, and why.

Expected: A table of assets, vectors and mitigations.

Most people are surprised how much of their attack surface consists of other companies and connected devices.

Done when:

  • Your table covers at least six items and five vector categories.
  • You chose a mitigation for each of the top three risks.

3. See it in real products

Supply chain risk reaches design through integrations and marketplaces. A plugin directory that shows who built an app, what it can access and when it was last reviewed is making third-party risk understandable to the people who install it.

In security operations

  • A managed service provider's remote tool is compromised, and ransomware reaches hundreds of its customers. That is one failure with a wide blast radius.
  • A printer with default credentials becomes an attacker's foothold on the corporate network.

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A security team sees PowerShell downloading and running code on a server. There are no malware files on disk. What technique does this suggest, and why is it hard to detect?

Compare your reasoning

Living off the land. The attacker is abusing a legitimate built-in tool, so simple file-based detection sees nothing unusual. Behaviour monitoring, command logging and restricting PowerShell help.

1. What does living off the land mean?

  • Using legitimate tools already on the system to carry out an attack
  • Physically breaking into a building
  • Only attacking agricultural companies
  • Buying a commercial exploit kit
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Using legitimate tools already on the system to carry out an attack. Abusing built-in tools helps avoid detection.
  • Incorrect: Physically breaking into a building. This is a physical attack.
  • Incorrect: Only attacking agricultural companies. The phrase has nothing to do with farming.
  • Incorrect: Buying a commercial exploit kit. The key idea is reusing what is already present.

2. Attackers compromise a provider that manages IT for many clients, and use its tools to reach those clients. What is this?

  • A supply chain-based attack through a managed service provider
  • A watering hole attack
  • A QR code attack
  • A race condition
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: A supply chain-based attack through a managed service provider. Trust in the provider is what is abused.
  • Incorrect: A watering hole attack. That relies on a website that targets visit.
  • Incorrect: A QR code attack. There is no QR code.
  • Incorrect: A race condition. That is a software timing flaw.

3. Attackers infect an industry association's website that staff of target companies regularly visit. What is this called?

  • Watering hole
  • Tailgating
  • Whaling
  • Shoulder surfing
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Watering hole. The attacker waits where the targets gather.
  • Incorrect: Tailgating. That is following someone through a door.
  • Incorrect: Whaling. That is phishing aimed at executives.
  • Incorrect: Shoulder surfing. That is watching someone's screen or keypad.

4. Match each example to its category.

Correct matches
  • A smart camera with default credentials: IoT-based
  • A cloned access badge: Physical-based
  • A tampered USB left in a parking lot: External media-based
  • A jammed wireless signal: Signal-based

Each example shows which route was used.

5. Why are operational technology systems particularly difficult to protect?

  • They control physical processes and often cannot be patched or restarted easily
  • They have no software at all
  • They cannot be connected to any network
  • They are only used by home users
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: They control physical processes and often cannot be patched or restarted easily. Availability and safety limit how they can be changed.
  • Incorrect: They have no software at all. They do run software.
  • Incorrect: They cannot be connected to any network. Many are connected.
  • Incorrect: They are only used by home users. They are used in industry and utilities.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Trusting signed updates without limit. A compromised vendor can sign malicious code.
  • Overlooking printers, cameras and sensors in asset lists.

Summary

  • Attackers often go around the strongest defenses.
  • Third parties extend your attack surface.
  • IoT, OT, physical and wireless routes need their own controls.
