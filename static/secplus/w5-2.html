Security controls: categories and types

Lesson 18 · Week 5 · Domain 1: General Security Concepts · Objective 1.1 · Beginner · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Professional relevance for cross-functional work

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Classify any control by category and by type.
  • Explain why one control can have more than one type.
  • Recommend a compensating control when the preferred control is not possible.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Exam questions regularly give a control and ask you to classify it. In real work, classification shows gaps, such as an organization with plenty of prevention and no way of detecting when prevention fails.

In plain language

Category describes how a control is carried out: through technology, management decisions, people's daily routines or physical things. Type describes what the control is trying to achieve: stop it, discourage it, notice it, fix it, tell people what to do, or stand in when something else cannot be used.

Take a house. The fence is physical, and it is preventive and deterring. The camera is physical or technical, and detective. The rule that the last person locks up is directive. Repairing a broken lock is corrective.

2. Technical deep dive

The four control categories are technical (also called logical), which are implemented in technology such as firewalls and encryption; managerial (also called administrative), which are decisions and documentation such as policies and risk assessments; operational, which are carried out by people, such as security guards or awareness training; and physical (also called environmental), such as locks, fences and fire suppression.

The six control types are preventive (stops an event), deterring (discourages an attempt), detective (identifies an event), corrective (restores after an event), compensating (an alternative when the primary control is not feasible) and directive (tells people what to do, such as a policy).

Category and type are separate questions, so each control has both. Context can change the type. A guard who physically stops someone is preventive, while one simply watching monitors is detective.

Key terms

Technical controlA control carried out by technology. Also called a logical control.
Managerial controlA control based on policy, oversight and risk decisions. Also called administrative.
Operational controlA control carried out by people following a routine.
Physical controlA tangible control, such as a lock or fence. Environmental controls such as fire suppression are grouped here.
PreventiveDesigned to stop an event from happening.
DeterringDesigned to discourage someone from trying.
DetectiveDesigned to identify that something happened.
CorrectiveDesigned to fix things and restore after an event.
CompensatingAn alternative used when the primary control cannot be applied.
DirectiveTells people what they must do, such as a policy.

Walkthrough: Classify a control in two steps

  1. Read: guards check badges at the data centre entrance.
  2. Category: who or what carries it out? People following a routine, so operational. A physical barrier would be physical.
  3. Type: what does it aim to achieve? Stopping entry, so preventive, and their presence also deters.
  4. Write both answers. Exams may ask for either one.

Hands-on lab

Audit a control set

About 35 min. A table in any editor

  1. List ten controls you see at work, school or home, physical and digital.
  2. Classify each by category and by type.
  3. Count your types and find the type you have least of.
  4. Propose one control to fill that gap.

Expected: A table with both classifications and a gap identified.

Many organizations are heavy in preventive controls and light on detective ones. If prevention fails silently, nobody knows.

Done when:

  • Each of your ten controls has both a category and a type.
  • You identified a gap and proposed a control.

3. See it in real products

Controls appear in products constantly. A required approval is preventive, an alert is detective, a one-click restore is corrective, and a warning banner is a deterrent. Naming them helps you work out whether a design is relying on only one kind of protection.

In security operations

  • An old medical device cannot be patched. The hospital isolates it on its own network segment and monitors traffic. That is a compensating control.
  • A company's backups are corrective, the alert that ransomware began is detective, and the rule forbidding personal USB drives is directive.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

No design exercise here, on purpose. The exercises on either side of this lesson apply these categories to real flows.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Which of these controls are enforced technically, and which only by policy?

Security:

  • Which control categories are we relying on for this feature, and what is the backstop?

Product:

  • Which controls do enterprise customers ask us to prove?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A manufacturer runs a critical machine on an unsupported operating system that cannot be patched or replaced for two years. Which type of control is most appropriate, and what might it look like?

Compare your reasoning

A compensating control. Patching is the primary control but is not feasible, so the company could place the machine on an isolated segment, allow only required traffic and monitor it closely. This is not a permanent fix, and the risk should be recorded.

1. A company publishes a policy forbidding password sharing. Which control category is this?

  • Managerial
  • Technical
  • Physical
  • Operational
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Managerial. Policies are a management decision.
  • Incorrect: Technical. No technology enforces this by itself.
  • Incorrect: Physical. This is not a tangible barrier.
  • Incorrect: Operational. Operational controls are carried out by people as a routine, while writing policy is managerial.

2. A sign says that the premises are under video surveillance. Which type of control is the sign?

  • Deterring
  • Detective
  • Corrective
  • Compensating
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Deterring. The sign aims to discourage an attempt.
  • Incorrect: Detective. The sign itself detects nothing; a camera recording would.
  • Incorrect: Corrective. The sign does not restore anything.
  • Incorrect: Compensating. It does not stand in for another control.

3. A system cannot support MFA, so administrators must connect through a monitored jump server with session recording. What type of control is this?

  • Compensating
  • Directive
  • Deterring
  • Corrective
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Compensating. It provides alternative protection because the primary control is not feasible.
  • Incorrect: Directive. A directive control instructs people but does not technically substitute.
  • Incorrect: Deterring. Its purpose is not just to discourage.
  • Incorrect: Corrective. It does not repair damage after an event.

4. Match each control to its main type.

Correct matches
  • Restoring a server from backup: Corrective
  • An intrusion detection alert: Detective
  • A locked server room door: Preventive
  • A mandatory procedure document: Directive

Ask what each control aims to accomplish.

5. Which option correctly classifies security awareness training that teaches staff to report phishing?

  • Operational category, with preventive and detective effects
  • Physical category, corrective type
  • Technical category, deterring type
  • Managerial category, compensating type
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Operational category, with preventive and detective effects. People carry it out, it helps stop attacks and it encourages reports of them.
  • Incorrect: Physical category, corrective type. It is not a tangible control and does not restore systems.
  • Incorrect: Technical category, deterring type. Training is delivered and carried out by people.
  • Incorrect: Managerial category, compensating type. It is not substituting for an unavailable control.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Mixing up category and type. Ask how it is done, and then what it aims to do.
  • Calling every alternative control compensating. A compensating control exists because the primary one is not feasible.

Summary

  • Categories are technical, managerial, operational and physical.
  • Types are preventive, deterring, detective, corrective, compensating and directive.
  • One control has a category and can have more than one type.
