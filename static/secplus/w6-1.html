Threat intelligence and how vulnerabilities are scored

Lesson 22 · Week 6 · Domain 2: Threats, Vulnerabilities, and Attacks · Objective 2.1 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Professional relevance for cross-functional work

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Use intelligence sources and threat feeds appropriately.
  • Read a CVE entry and a CVSS score.
  • Prioritize vulnerabilities using more than a score alone.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

No organization can fix everything at once. Threat intelligence and scoring help teams spend limited time on what is most likely to hurt them.

In plain language

Weather forecasts help you decide whether to carry an umbrella. Threat intelligence is a forecast for attacks. A CVE number is like a catalogue number for one specific known flaw, and a CVSS score is a severity rating for that flaw.

2. Technical deep dive

A threat has a likelihood, how probable it is, and an impact, how harmful it would be. The threat life cycle describes how a threat emerges, becomes active and declines, and intelligence itself follows a cycle of direction, collection, analysis, dissemination and feedback.

Intelligence sources include open-source intelligence (OSINT), commercial providers, government advisories, industry groups such as Information Sharing and Analysis Centers (ISACs), and internal logs. Threat feeds deliver indicators such as malicious IP addresses and file hashes, often in machine-readable formats. Feeds lose value quickly, so age and context matter.

Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures (CVE) assigns a unique ID to a publicly known vulnerability, such as CVE-2021-44228. The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) scores severity from 0.0 to 10.0. CVSS measures severity and does not alone measure your risk. Vulnerability types describe the kind of weakness, such as those in the Common Weakness Enumeration (CWE), for example injection or buffer overflow.

Prioritization combines severity with context: is the system exposed to the internet, is the flaw being actively exploited, how important is the asset, and what controls already exist? CISA's Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog lists flaws seen in real attacks, which is strong evidence for acting quickly.

Walkthrough: Prioritize three findings

  1. List each finding with its CVSS score.
  2. Check exposure: can it be reached from the internet?
  3. Check exploitation: is it in the CISA KEV catalog?
  4. Check asset value: what would losing this system cost?
  5. Rank by combining all these, and record your reasoning.

Hands-on lab

Read real vulnerability records

About 35 min. NVD and the CISA KEV catalog in a browser

  1. Search the CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog and open one entry from the last year.
  2. Find the same CVE in the National Vulnerability Database. Record its CVSS score and vector.
  3. Write down the affected product, the required action and the weakness type (CWE).
  4. Imagine this product runs on your public web server. Write a two-sentence justification for its priority.

Expected: You have a record linking a CVE to its score, its weakness type and evidence of real exploitation.

Public databases like these are the same sources professionals use for daily triage.

Done when:

  • You recorded the CVE ID, score, CWE and required action.
  • Your priority statement uses at least one factor other than the CVSS score.

3. See it in real products

Severity scores become interface decisions in vulnerability dashboards. A CVSS score alone rarely tells a team what to fix first, which is why the Week 11 exercise asks you to design for prioritization and not merely for display.

In security operations

  • A CVSS 9.8 flaw on an isolated test server can be less urgent than a CVSS 7.5 flaw being actively exploited on an internet-facing payment system.
  • A feed flags an IP address as malicious, but that address is shared cloud infrastructure, and blocking it would stop legitimate business. Context prevents an outage.

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A scanner reports a CVSS 9.1 flaw on an internal lab server with no sensitive data, and a CVSS 7.8 flaw on the internet-facing customer portal listed in CISA's KEV catalog. Which should be fixed first?

Compare your reasoning

The customer portal. It is exposed, the flaw is known to be exploited, and the asset is important. CVSS measures severity, not the risk in this specific environment.

1. What does a CVE ID identify?

  • A specific publicly known vulnerability
  • A type of malware family
  • A company's security policy
  • An attacker's IP address
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: A specific publicly known vulnerability. Each CVE ID refers to one disclosed flaw.
  • Incorrect: A type of malware family. Malware names are not CVE IDs.
  • Incorrect: A company's security policy. A policy is an internal document.
  • Incorrect: An attacker's IP address. An IP address is an indicator of compromise.

2. Which statement about CVSS is correct?

  • It rates technical severity but does not by itself measure an organization's risk
  • A score of 10 means a system has already been breached
  • A higher score always means it must be fixed first
  • It is used to rate employee awareness
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: It rates technical severity but does not by itself measure an organization's risk. Context such as exposure and asset value also matters.
  • Incorrect: A score of 10 means a system has already been breached. A score describes severity, not whether compromise has happened.
  • Incorrect: A higher score always means it must be fixed first. Context can change the order.
  • Incorrect: It is used to rate employee awareness. CVSS scores vulnerabilities.

3. An analyst uses posts on public forums and news stories to research a group. Which type of source is this?

  • OSINT
  • A proprietary threat feed
  • Internal audit data
  • A CVSS vector
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: OSINT. These are public sources.
  • Incorrect: A proprietary threat feed. A proprietary feed is purchased.
  • Incorrect: Internal audit data. This originates inside the organization.
  • Incorrect: A CVSS vector. A vector describes how a score was built.

4. Which finding should usually be remediated first?

  • CVSS 7.5, internet-facing, listed as actively exploited
  • CVSS 9.0, on an isolated lab system with no data
  • CVSS 4.0, on an internal printer
  • CVSS 8.0, on a server scheduled to be retired today
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: CVSS 7.5, internet-facing, listed as actively exploited. Exposure and active exploitation raise the real urgency.
  • Incorrect: CVSS 9.0, on an isolated lab system with no data. High severity, but low exposure and impact.
  • Incorrect: CVSS 4.0, on an internal printer. Low severity and low exposure.
  • Incorrect: CVSS 8.0, on a server scheduled to be retired today. Retiring it soon removes the exposure.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Fixing strictly in order of CVSS score.
  • Treating every feed entry as accurate and current.

Summary

  • Intelligence guides priorities, and it ages quickly.
  • CVE identifies a flaw, and CVSS rates its severity.
  • Prioritize by severity, exposure, active exploitation and asset value.
