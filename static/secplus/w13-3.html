Third-party risk

Lesson 53 · Week 13 · Domain 5: Security Program Management and Oversight · Objective 5.3 · Beginner · about 100 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Direct product design relevance

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Follow a vendor from selection through monitoring.
  • Distinguish common agreements.
  • Recognize limits on third-party assessment.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Your suppliers' weaknesses become yours. A contract signed without due diligence can move risk into your organization unnoticed.

In plain language

Hiring a vendor is like hiring a contractor to work inside your home. You get quotes, check references, sign a contract that spells out the work and keep an eye on the job.

2. Technical deep dive

Vendor selection uses a request for proposal (RFP), request for information (RFI), request for quote (RFQ) and expression of interest (EOI). Due diligence evaluates a vendor before signing. Conflict of interest occurs when someone's personal interests could influence the decision.

Agreement types: a service-level agreement (SLA) defines measurable service commitments, and a service-level objective (SLO) is a specific target within it. A memorandum of understanding (MOU) records a general, usually non-binding, intent. A memorandum of agreement (MOA) is more specific about responsibilities. A non-disclosure agreement (NDA) protects confidential information. A master services agreement (MSA) sets general terms for future work. A statement of work (SOW) details specific deliverables.

Vendor monitoring includes right to audit, service-level monitoring, a vendor registry, vendor assessment, compliance attestation and penetration testing. Limitations and constraints include staffing limitations, resource availability, environment, legal and regulatory factors, geography/jurisdiction, financial/return on investment (ROI), vendor lock-in and assurance mechanisms. Rules of engagement define what testers may and may not do.

Walkthrough: Onboard a new SaaS vendor

  1. Use an RFP to compare options.
  2. Run due diligence and review security attestations.
  3. Sign an MSA and SLA, with data handling terms and right to audit.
  4. Add it to the vendor registry and assign an owner.
  5. Monitor service levels and reassess periodically.

Hands-on lab

Conduct a vendor assessment

About 30 min. Worksheet and a public vendor trust page

  1. Choose a well-known SaaS company with a public security or trust page.
  2. Note its certifications, data location and breach notification commitments.
  3. Score it across five questions you write yourself.
  4. List what you could not find and would need to ask.

Expected: A one-page assessment with gaps identified.

Marketing claims differ from evidence. Noting what is missing is as valuable as what is present.

Done when:

  • You answered all five questions with evidence or noted a gap.
  • You listed at least two follow-up questions.

3. See it in real products

Vendor risk workflows are usually spreadsheets and email chains, and customers evaluating your product go through the same process in reverse. A trust center that shares certifications and answers on request is a product experience, and so is the internal tool that decides whether a new vendor gets customer data.

In security operations

  • An MSA sets the general terms, and each project gets its own SOW.
  • A contract includes the right to audit, so a customer can verify that security promises are being kept.

4. Apply it as a Product Designer

Design brief: Design a vendor security review workflow

About 45 min

Scenario: A growing company wants to replace its emailed spreadsheet process for reviewing software vendors. Any employee can currently sign up for a tool with company data.

Problem statement: Nobody knows which vendors hold what data, reviews are slow, and approved vendors are never checked again.

Your design task:

  • Design intake that asks a requester about data and access in plain language.
  • Design risk tiering that decides how deep the review goes.
  • Design the evidence and contract checklist, including the right to audit.
  • Design monitoring, renewal and offboarding.

Technical security constraints:

  • A vendor's assurance documents can be out of date or limited in scope.
  • Offboarding must include return or deletion of data.
  • Review effort must match risk, or staff will bypass it.

User needs:

  • Employees need a quick answer on low-risk tools.
  • Security and legal staff need one source of truth.
  • Leaders need to see overall vendor exposure.

Expected deliverable: An intake and tiering flow, a review dashboard and a vendor life cycle diagram.

Evaluation criteria:

  • Evidence, contract terms and offboarding are covered.
  • The design considers shadow IT, stale assessments and fourth parties.
  • A requester with no security background can finish the intake.
  • The process is tuned so that the secure path is also the easy path.

Senior-level considerations:

  • If the approved route is slow, people will avoid it, so treat turnaround time as a security measure.
  • Legal owns contract language, so design the hand-off and leave the wording to them.

A design review is not a security sign-off. Have security engineers confirm technical decisions.

Questions a Senior Product Designer should ask

Engineering:

  • Which vendors currently process customer data, and for what?

Security:

  • What evidence do we require from a vendor, and how often is it refreshed?

Product:

  • Which sales deals have been slowed by security questionnaires?

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: A company wants a document that details the exact tasks and deliverables a consultant will produce for a single project, under an existing general agreement. Which document fits?

Compare your reasoning

A statement of work (SOW), which sits under the master services agreement (MSA).

1. Which document protects confidential information shared between parties?

  • NDA
  • SLA
  • RFQ
  • SOW
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: NDA. It restricts disclosure.
  • Incorrect: SLA. This defines service commitments.
  • Incorrect: RFQ. This requests pricing.
  • Incorrect: SOW. This defines deliverables.

2. Match each to its purpose.

Correct matches
  • SLA: Measurable service commitments
  • MSA: General terms for future work
  • SOW: Specific project deliverables
  • MOU: General statement of intent

These agreements operate at different levels of specificity.

3. Which contract clause allows a customer to examine a vendor's controls?

  • Right to audit
  • Rules of engagement
  • Vendor lock-in
  • Expression of interest
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Right to audit. It gives inspection rights.
  • Incorrect: Rules of engagement. These define penetration test limits.
  • Incorrect: Vendor lock-in. This is a constraint, not a clause.
  • Incorrect: Expression of interest. This is an early purchasing document.

4. Before a third party tests a system, what must be agreed?

  • Rules of engagement, including scope and authorization
  • Only the price
  • The tester's favourite tools
  • Nothing, if the tester is experienced
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Rules of engagement, including scope and authorization. Testing without these can be illegal or harmful.
  • Incorrect: Only the price. This omits scope and authorization.
  • Incorrect: The tester's favourite tools. This is not the primary concern.
  • Incorrect: Nothing, if the tester is experienced. Authorization is always needed.

B. Product design assessment

Rate your work in the interactive app, where it counts toward design competency only.

1. A vendor shares a SOC 2 report from two years ago. What should the workflow prompt?

  • Request a current report and check that its scope fits
  • Accept it, because a SOC 2 report never loses its value
  • Reject every vendor that has ever shared an older report
  • Treat the report as proof that the vendor cannot be breached
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Request a current report and check that its scope fits. Assurance can go stale, and it may not cover the service you use.
  • Incorrect: Accept it, because a SOC 2 report never loses its value. Reports describe a period and must be kept up to date.
  • Incorrect: Reject every vendor that has ever shared an older report. This is excessive, because a current report may simply exist.
  • Incorrect: Treat the report as proof that the vendor cannot be breached. No report can provide that guarantee.

Common mistakes

Summary

  • Agreements differ in specificity.
  • Monitor vendors throughout the relationship.
