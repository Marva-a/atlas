Governance documents: guidelines, standards, procedures, plans and policies

Lesson 51 · Week 13 · Domain 5: Security Program Management and Oversight · Objective 5.1 · Beginner · about 100 min

Open this lesson in Atlas →

Professional relevance for cross-functional work

1. Learn the concept

By the end, you can

  • Tell apart policies, standards, procedures and guidelines.
  • Identify common security policies.
  • Choose the right document for a situation.

Why it exists and what problem it solves

Security decisions must be written down and enforceable. Exam questions often test whether you can tell a policy from a standard or a procedure.

In plain language

A policy says what must happen: all staff must wear seatbelts. A standard gives measurable requirements: the seatbelt must meet this rating. A procedure gives the steps: buckle it this way. A guideline gives recommended advice: consider adjusting your seat first.

2. Technical deep dive

Guidelines are recommended, not mandatory, and include benchmarks, advisories, implementation guides and reference architecture. Standards are mandatory and measurable, and include baselines, passwords, physical security, encryption and technical specifications such as a request for comments (RFC).

Procedures are step-by-step instructions, such as a standard operating procedure (SOP) or a runbook. Plans include business continuity and disaster recovery. Policies set high-level expectations, including bring your own device (BYOD), acceptable use policy (AUP), clean desk, information security, incident response, data classification and retention, access control, data disposal, vulnerability disclosure and privacy.

Walkthrough: Classify a sentence

  1. All laptops must be encrypted: policy.
  2. Laptops must use AES-256 full disk encryption: standard.
  3. Click Settings, then Encryption, then Enable: procedure.
  4. We suggest backing up before encrypting: guideline.

Hands-on lab

Evaluate a policy

About 30 min. Worksheet and a public acceptable use policy

  1. Find a publicly posted acceptable use policy from a university.
  2. Mark sentences that are really standards, procedures or guidelines.
  3. Identify one vague requirement and rewrite it to be measurable.
  4. State who enforces it and how.

Expected: A marked-up policy and one improved requirement.

Policies fail when they are vague or nobody owns enforcement.

Done when:

  • You classified at least five sentences.
  • Your rewritten requirement can be measured.

3. See it in real products

Policies become product requirements. An organization's password standard or acceptable use policy may need settings, enforcement and reporting in your product. Knowing the difference between a policy, a standard and a procedure helps you ask the right person.

In security operations

  • A policy requires strong encryption, a standard specifies AES-256, and a procedure shows how to enable it on laptops.
  • A clean desk policy keeps printed customer information from being left out overnight.

5. Test your understanding

A. Security+ technical assessment

Exam-style scenario: An administrator needs step-by-step instructions to rotate a server's certificate. Which document type should this be?

Compare your reasoning

A procedure, such as an SOP or runbook, since it gives specific steps.

1. Which document gives step-by-step instructions?

  • Procedure
  • Policy
  • Guideline
  • Benchmark
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: Procedure. Procedures state how to do a task.
  • Incorrect: Policy. A policy states what is expected.
  • Incorrect: Guideline. Guidelines recommend.
  • Incorrect: Benchmark. A benchmark is a reference.

2. Which of these is a standard?

  • All laptops must use AES-256 full disk encryption
  • We recommend using encryption
  • Click Enable under Settings
  • The company cares about security
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: All laptops must use AES-256 full disk encryption. It is specific and measurable.
  • Incorrect: We recommend using encryption. This is a guideline.
  • Incorrect: Click Enable under Settings. This is a procedure.
  • Incorrect: The company cares about security. This is a general statement.

3. Match each need to a policy.

Correct matches
  • Rules on personal phones at work: BYOD
  • What staff may do with company systems: AUP
  • Papers must be stored away overnight: Clean desk
  • How outside researchers can report flaws: Vulnerability disclosure

Each policy addresses a specific behaviour.

4. Which statement about guidelines is correct?

  • They are recommended, not mandatory
  • They override policies
  • They are legally binding in all cases
  • They are step-by-step instructions
Answer and explanation
  • Correct: They are recommended, not mandatory. Guidelines offer advice.
  • Incorrect: They override policies. Policies carry greater authority.
  • Incorrect: They are legally binding in all cases. They are generally not.
  • Incorrect: They are step-by-step instructions. Those are procedures.

6. Review and references

Common mistakes

  • Calling everything a policy.
  • Writing a policy without an owner.

Summary

  • Policies state what, standards specify, procedures show how, and guidelines suggest.
  • Every document needs an owner and a review cycle.
