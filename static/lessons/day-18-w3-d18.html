Exercise 7: SSO sign-in and device posture

Day 18 · Week 3 · Intermediate · about 120 min

You will see how a company’s identity provider and device checks feed into network access decisions.

By the end, you can: Walk through an SSO sign-in with a device-posture check and describe what the product evaluated before granting access.

Why it matters to a designer

In real companies, access depends on two things at once: the person (from the identity provider) and the device (from posture checks). Designers must explain both when someone is blocked, and help admins understand which signal caused a decision.

Key ideas

  • Single sign-on means one company account opens many apps. Tailscale does not store passwords; it trusts an identity provider such as Google Workspace, Microsoft Entra or Okta.
  • When someone leaves and their identity provider account is disabled, they lose network access too. Identity and network are now one system.
  • Device posture is what the system knows about the device: operating system version, whether it is managed by the company, whether its disk is encrypted.
  • A posture rule example: “only devices running a supported macOS version with the company’s management tool may reach production”.
  • The design challenge: a blocked employee needs to know which check failed and how to fix it, in plain words. “Access denied” alone creates a support ticket.

Key terms

single sign-on (SSO)One sign-in with a company account opens many apps.
managed deviceA device controlled by the company’s IT tools.
MDMMobile device management: tools that manage and check company devices.
posture checkA rule that looks at the device’s state before allowing access.
signalA piece of information used in an access decision, such as user group or OS version.

What a good answer looks like

  • They lose it, because access depends on the identity provider. This is why SSO plus provisioning is a big part of offboarding safely.
  • For example, operating system version and whether the device is company-managed. Rule: “only managed devices on a supported OS version may reach production”.
  • It says which check failed and what to do, without blame. For example: “Your Mac needs the latest macOS update before you can reach production. Update in System Settings, then try again.”
