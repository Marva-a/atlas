Logs, flow logs, alerts and the people who read them

Day 36 · Week 6 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Logs are the records of what happened; flow logs record connections, audit logs record changes, and alerts are the few events that need a person’s attention now.

By the end, you can: Distinguish event logs, audit records and flow logs, and identify which role in an organization reads each.

Why it matters to a designer

Security teams spend their days in logs and alert queues, and most products show these badly: too much noise, too little context. Designing for them is a big opportunity, and it builds directly on the flow you saw in Exercise 12.

Key ideas

  • From Week 1 you know “accounting”: recording what happened. Logs are that record at scale.
  • A flow log records a connection: who talked to whom, when, on which port, and whether it was allowed. It does not record the content of the conversation.
  • An audit log records changes: who changed which setting, rule or user, and when. Example: “Alex changed the policy file at 14:02”.
  • Identity events worth watching: many failed sign-ins, a sign-in from a new country, MFA turned off, a new admin created, and access granted to sensitive systems.
  • A security operations team (SOC) watches alerts and investigates. Analysts get hundreds of alerts a day, and most are false alarms.
  • Alert fatigue: when there are too many alerts, people stop trusting them and miss the real one. Every alert should be actionable, rare enough to matter and clear about what to do.

Key terms

logA record of events, written by a system as they happen.
flow logA record of network connections: source, destination, port, time and allowed or denied.
audit logA record of who changed what, and when.
alertAn event that a system decides needs a person’s attention.
SOCSecurity operations centre: the team that watches for and responds to threats.
false positiveAn alert about something that is actually harmless.
alert fatigueMissing important alerts because there are too many.

What a good answer looks like

  • A flow log records connections (who talked to whom and whether it was allowed). An audit log records changes to the system (who changed which setting and when).
  • For example: many failed sign-ins for one account, a sign-in from an unusual place, MFA being turned off, a new admin being created, or access granted to a sensitive system.
  • It is actionable, rare enough to be trusted, and clear: what happened, why it matters, and what to do next, with the context needed to decide.
