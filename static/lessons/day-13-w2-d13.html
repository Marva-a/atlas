Onboarding teardown and threat model: write it up and share

Day 13 · Week 2 · Basics · about 230 min

You will turn this week's work into a short teardown with before and after, plus a one-page threat model of the same flow, so others can see how you think about usability and security together.

By the end, you can: Write and share an evidence-based teardown of a networking product's onboarding with a one-page threat model of the same flow: a data-flow diagram, STRIDE threats at its trust boundaries, ranked risks and three security requirements expressed as design changes.

Why it matters to a designer

Hiring managers in this domain look for designers who can explain technical products simply and back decisions with evidence. A short teardown shows that; a short, honest threat model of the same flow shows you can reason about systems, risk and users together, which very few design portfolios do.

Key ideas

  • Structure: the product and the user, the task, what happened (with screenshots and time), the three main problems, your redesign, and what you would test next.
  • Use the user’s words for problems (“I didn’t know if it worked”) and the right terms for the solution (activation, contextual help).
  • Be fair to the product: say what it does well. Many readers will work there.
  • Explain one technical idea simply (for example, why devices behind NAT are hard to connect) to show you understand the domain.
  • Then the threat model. On Day 4 you modelled a sign-in in miniature; now model the flow you tore down (signing up, adding a device, sharing it) with the four questions: What are we working on? What can go wrong? What are we going to do about it? Did we do a good job?
  • Start with the picture: a data-flow diagram like the one from Day 8, with trust boundaries drawn. Threats cluster at the boundaries.
  • Use STRIDE (Day 4) per boundary: spoofing (pretending to be someone), tampering (changing data), repudiation (denying an action), information disclosure (leaks), denial of service (outages) and elevation of privilege (gaining more access).
  • Rank as on Day 4's risk lesson: likelihood and impact, roughly. Pick the top three; a short list that gets acted on beats a long list that does not.
  • Respond as on Day 6: layered controls, secure defaults and testable requirements. Note at least one control that is a design change.
  • Label your work honestly: a self-initiated model of a product you do not work on is still valuable, as long as you say what you assumed and could not verify.
  • Look back at the week: which words are now easy, and which still feel vague? Add the vague ones to your glossary.

Key terms

before and afterShowing the current design next to your proposed one, with reasons.
write-upA short written case of what you did, why, and what you learned.
next testWhat you would check with real users to see if your design works.
repudiationBeing able to deny having done something because there is no reliable record.
elevation of privilegeGaining more access than you were given.
abuse caseA description of how someone could misuse a feature, written like a user story for an attacker.
assumptionSomething a threat model takes as true but has not verified; it should be written down.

Today's steps

Write the teardown, 500–800 words, using the structure above.

50 min

Place your before and after frames and annotated screenshots in the write-up.

25 min

Read OWASP's short threat modelling page to refresh the process. — Threat Modeling (OWASP)

10 min

Draw the data-flow diagram of the onboarding flow you tore down (signing up, adding a device, sharing it) with its trust boundaries, by hand or in OWASP Threat Dragon. — OWASP Threat Dragon (OWASP)

25 min

At each trust boundary, list STRIDE threats. Aim for at least eight in total, and write two as abuse cases ('As someone whose share was removed, I want to keep reaching the device so that...').

25 min

Rank the threats by likelihood and impact and pick the top three. For each, propose layered controls and write one security requirement as a design change (a default, a confirmation, an expiry, a visible log).

25 min

Write the one-page threat model and add it to the teardown as a security section: scope, diagram, top risks, requirements and assumptions.

20 min

Ask one person, ideally an engineer, to read it and tell you which part of the teardown or the threat model was unclear.

15 min

Post a short version, or save it in your portfolio folder.

15 min

Update your glossary and your people map with anything you learned this week.

15 min

Skip or skim

Do not turn it into a full case study, and skip attack trees and formal threat-modelling tools. Short and clear beats long and complete: a reader should get the teardown and the threat model in ten minutes.

Check yourself

  • What is the one problem from your teardown you would fix first, and why?
  • Explain in two sentences, for a non-technical friend, what Tailscale does.
  • Where on your diagram did most threats appear, and why?
  • Which of your top three risks did you choose to reduce with a design change, and what was the change?
  • What assumptions did your threat model make that you would need to verify with the product's engineers?

What a good answer looks like

  • A good answer picks the problem that most delays or blocks activation and backs it with evidence from your own run, such as the time lost or the moment of doubt.
  • For example: “It connects your devices into your own private network, so they can reach each other securely from anywhere. You sign in with your work account, and the company decides who can reach what.”
  • Usually at trust boundaries: where the contractor's browser reaches the app, where invitations go out by email, and where the app grants access to project data. That is where untrusted input and identity checks meet.
  • For example: contractor access expires by default after the project end date, with a reminder to the admin. It reduces the risk of a stale external account (Day 4) keeping access after the work ends.
  • For example: that invitation links expire, that removing a user ends their active sessions, that exports respect project permissions, or that admin actions are logged. Listing them shows honesty and gives engineers concrete questions.
