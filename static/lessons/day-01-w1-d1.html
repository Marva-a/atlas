Welcome: identity, access and what security protects

Day 1 · Week 1 · Basics · about 125 min

Identity and access security answers three everyday questions (who are you, what may you do, and how do you get there safely?), and all of it exists to keep valuable things confidential, correct and available.

By the end, you can: Explain identity, access and accounting using the hotel example, name what Okta, Tailscale and Tigera each mainly handle, and say for an asset whether a failure would hurt its confidentiality, integrity or availability.

Why it matters to a designer

Every product in this field, from Okta to Tailscale to Tigera, is a different answer to those three questions, and every security team decides what to protect first by asking which asset matters and which property would fail. With both lenses the field stops looking like a wall of acronyms, and you can argue for a design in the team's own terms.

Key ideas

  • Think of a hotel. At the front desk you show ID (who you are). You get a key card that opens only your room and the gym (what you may do). The hotel keeps a record of when each card was used (what happened). Computer security works the same way.
  • Identity: knowing who someone (or something, like a server) is. Example: signing in to your work email with your password and a code on your phone.
  • Access: deciding what that someone may do. Example: a designer can open Figma and the design drive, but not the payroll system.
  • Network: how you reach the thing safely, from home, a café or the office. Example: Tailscale lets your laptop reach a company server privately from anywhere.
  • What all of this protects: an asset is anything worth protecting, such as customer data, money, a company's reputation, a service that must stay up, even an admin's time. Security work starts by listing assets, not tools.
  • Security protects three properties of an asset, the CIA triad. Confidentiality: only the right people can see it (only HR reads salaries; a breach is a leak). Integrity: it stays correct and only the right people change it (no one quietly edits an invoice's bank details; a breach is tampering). Availability: people can use it when they need it (a breach is an outage).
  • The three pull against each other. Locking an account after five wrong passwords protects confidentiality but lets an attacker lock real users out, hurting availability. Good design finds the balance for the asset at stake.
  • Not every asset needs the same protection. A public marketing page needs integrity (no defacement) and availability, but not confidentiality. Matching protection to the asset is the start of risk thinking (Day 4).
  • The companies: identity providers (Okta, Microsoft Entra, Keycloak), password managers (1Password), network access (Tailscale, Cloudflare), cloud and Kubernetes security (Tigera, Wiz). You will meet all of these types in this course.
  • Why designers matter here: many security failures are design failures. People reuse passwords because rules are painful; admins give too much access because the right setting is hard to find; alerts are ignored because there are too many.
  • How this course works: each day has a lesson in plain words, a few short references and a small task. Weeks 1–2 are basics, Weeks 3–6 go deeper, Weeks 7–8 are advanced, and you finish with two portfolio case studies.

Key terms

identityWho a person or a machine is, as a computer knows it, usually an account.
accessWhat an identity is allowed to reach or do.
accountThe record a system keeps about one person or machine: name, sign-in details, permissions.
identity provider (IdP)The service that signs people in and tells other apps who they are, such as Okta or Google.
IAMIdentity and access management: the tools and work of managing who can access what.
assetAnything of value that needs protecting: data, money, systems, reputation.
confidentialityOnly authorised people can see the information.
integrityInformation and systems stay correct and are changed only by the right people, in the right way.
availabilityPeople can use the system and its information when they need to.
CIA triadConfidentiality, integrity and availability: the three properties security protects.
data classificationLabelling information by how sensitive it is, such as public, internal, confidential or restricted.

What a good answer looks like

  • The ID at the front desk proves who you are (identity). The key card opens only some doors (access). The hotel’s log of when the card was used is the record (accounting). Software does the same for accounts, apps and data.
  • For example: people reuse simple passwords because the rules are painful; admins give too much access because the right setting is hard to find; or important alerts are missed because there are too many.
  • Okta signs people in and manages their accounts (identity). Tailscale connects people and devices privately and decides who can reach what (network access). Tigera controls and shows traffic between services in Kubernetes clusters (cloud network security).
  • Confidentiality: password hashes or session tokens leak, so someone else can read them. Integrity: an attacker changes a user's recovery email without permission. Availability: the sign-in service is down, or users are locked out, so nobody can work.
  • It protects confidentiality by slowing down password guessing. It can harm availability, because an attacker who knows a username can lock the real person out on purpose. Rate limits, delays or step-up checks can balance the two.
  • Because protection should match what is at stake. Without knowing which assets matter and which property matters for each, teams spend effort on the wrong things and miss what would really hurt.
