Protecting traffic and data: TLS, firewalls, VPNs, encryption, hashing and privacy

Day 9 · Week 2 · Basics · about 200 min

TLS keeps traffic private, firewalls decide what may pass, NAT lets devices share an address and a VPN creates a private path; stored data needs its own protection, passwords are hashed, keys are the real secret, and privacy asks a different question from security.

By the end, you can: Explain what TLS does and does not guarantee, how a firewall, NAT and a VPN each work and why a VPN alone does not grant app-level access, how encryption at rest differs from hashing, and give one example of something secure but not private.

Why it matters to a designer

Products like Tailscale exist because traditional VPNs and firewalls are hard to set up and easy to get wrong; if you understand them, you can explain the product's value in one sentence. Designers also decide what a screen shows, what an export contains and how long logs are kept, so knowing how data is protected lets you push for collecting less, showing less and deleting on time.

Key ideas

  • TLS is the lock icon: it encrypts traffic between your browser and a site (confidentiality) and detects tampering (integrity). When certificate validation succeeds, it also proves you are talking to the real server, not an impostor. HTTPS is HTTP sent inside TLS.
  • A firewall is a guard that checks each connection against rules: who may come in, to which port. Example: “only the office may reach port 22”.
  • NAT lets many devices in a home share one public address. Good for saving addresses, but it makes it hard for two devices behind different routers to reach each other directly. NAT is not a security boundary; a firewall is.
  • A traditional VPN connects your device to a central gateway, like a single front door into the office network. Everything goes through the hub, even traffic between two people at home. Being on the VPN is not the same as being authorized: the VPN gets you to the network, and a separate check still decides what you may reach.
  • A mesh VPN like Tailscale connects devices directly to each other (built on WireGuard), with a coordination server that hands out keys and rules. It feels like all your devices are on one private network.
  • For users, the classic VPN pain is: slow, “am I connected?”, and once you are in, you can reach far too much. Zero trust products answer that last problem, which you will study next week.
  • Beyond the network: TLS is encryption in transit. Encryption at rest protects stored data (disks, databases, backups) if the storage is stolen. You usually need both, and neither protects data from someone with legitimate access who misuses it.
  • Encryption is reversible with the right key. Hashing is one-way: you cannot get the input back. Passwords are stored as slow, salted hashes so even the company cannot read them; at sign-in, the typed password is hashed and compared. So 'we will email you your password' is a red flag.
  • Keys are the real secret. Strong encryption with a key stored next to the data is weak. Key management covers where keys live, who can use them, rotation and revocation. Cloud key management services and hardware security modules exist for this.
  • Secrets include API keys, tokens and certificates. Treat them like keys: least privilege, short lifetimes, rotation, never in code or screenshots. Day 50 goes deeper.
  • Data minimisation: the safest data is data you never collected or already deleted. Retention rules say how long each kind of data is kept; deletion must include backups and copies within a stated time.
  • Privacy is about appropriate use of personal data: what is collected, why, who sees it and whether people understand and agree. A system can be secure and still violate privacy, for example by securely storing far more data than it needs and using it for an unexpected purpose.

Key terms

TLSEncryption that keeps traffic between two computers private; HTTPS uses it.
firewallSoftware or a device that allows or blocks connections based on rules.
NATNetwork address translation: many devices sharing one public address.
VPNVirtual private network: a private, encrypted path across a public network.
hub-and-spokeA design where all traffic goes through one central point.
meshA design where devices connect directly to each other.
WireGuardA modern, simple VPN protocol that Tailscale is built on.
encryption at restProtecting stored data so it is unreadable if the storage is taken.
hashingA one-way transformation of data; used for passwords so the original cannot be recovered.
saltRandom data added to each password before hashing, so identical passwords produce different hashes.
key managementHow encryption keys are created, stored, used, rotated and revoked.
data minimisationCollecting and keeping only the data that is needed for a clear purpose.
retentionHow long a kind of data is kept before it must be deleted.
privacyAppropriate collection and use of personal data, with transparency and choice for the people it describes.
personal dataInformation that identifies a person or can be linked to them.

What a good answer looks like

  • It protects traffic in transit from being read or changed by someone in the middle. It does not protect you from a malicious site, a stolen password or a compromised device.
  • Each device sits behind a router that shares one public address and blocks unexpected incoming connections, so neither device has an address the other can simply call.
  • A traditional VPN sends everyone through one central door into the whole network; a mesh connects your devices directly to each other, so it is faster and each connection can be limited by rules.
  • It protects the data on the disk from being read by the thief, because they do not have the key. It does not protect against someone who has the unlocked laptop, a logged-in user misusing data, or data already synced to other places.
  • Because the service never needs to read a password back, only to check it. A one-way, salted, slow hash lets it check a typed password without storing anything that could be turned back into the password if the database leaks.
  • For example: a well-protected analytics system that keeps every user's location history forever and uses it for advertising nobody was told about. It may be hard to break into, but it collects and uses more personal data than needed, without clear consent.
