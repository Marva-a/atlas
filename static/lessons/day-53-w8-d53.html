Case study 2: concepts, flows and prototype

Day 53 · Week 8 · Part of the Network and Kubernetes Security pathway · Advanced · about 120 min

You will design a flow that takes a platform engineer from observed traffic to a recommended policy, staged and previewed before it is enforced.

By the end, you can: Develop a concept, user flow and prototype for a network-policy recommendation experience.

Why it matters to a designer

This is the core of your second case study and the most technical design in the course. It shows you can design for engineers on a hard, real problem at a company like Tigera, combining traffic visualisation, recommendations and safe rollout.

Key ideas

  • Start from your problem statement, journey and Exercise 12 notes. The fear to design against: “If I enforce this, what breaks?”
  • Three concepts to compare: (1) a traffic map where you select connections to allow, (2) recommendations listed by namespace with confidence, (3) a staged-policy timeline showing what would have been blocked.
  • Show the policy both as plain language and as YAML, because engineers will want to check and copy the real thing.
  • Staged rollout: apply in “would block” mode, watch for a few days, review what would have been blocked, then enforce, with an easy way back.
  • Include hard states: a recommendation based on too little traffic, traffic that is suspicious and should not be allowed, and a policy that conflicts with an existing one.
  • Build a clickable prototype of the main flow.

Key terms

recommendation confidenceHow sure a system is about a suggestion, often based on how much data it saw.
staged rolloutApplying a change gradually or in a report-only mode before full effect.
blast radiusHow much could be affected if something goes wrong.
YAML viewShowing the real configuration text next to a visual view.

Check yourself

  • How does your design reduce the fear of breaking apps?
  • Why show both plain language and YAML?

What a good answer looks like

  • For example by staging policies before enforcing, showing exactly what would have been blocked, flagging low-confidence recommendations, and making rollback easy.
  • Plain language helps everyone understand the effect quickly; YAML lets engineers check the exact rule, review it in git and trust that nothing is hidden.
