The people you design for, and the people you work with

Day 5 · Week 1 · Basics · about 120 min

Security products have several kinds of users, and the person who buys the product is often not the person who uses it every day.

By the end, you can: List the distinct stakeholders in a security product (end users, admins, buyers, security teams) and explain why their goals can conflict.

Why it matters to a designer

In consumer apps one person signs up, uses and pays. In identity and network security, an IT admin sets things up, employees just want to get to work, a security lead worries about risk, and a CISO signs the contract. Good designs help each of them, and interviewers will ask how you balanced them.

Key ideas

  • The IT admin sets up and runs the tool: adds people, connects apps, answers “I can’t get in” tickets. Example: Priya, the only IT person at a 60-person startup, doing it between other jobs.
  • The security engineer decides the rules and investigates when something looks wrong. They want to be sure, and they read logs.
  • The platform or DevOps engineer connects servers, clusters and pipelines. They prefer a command line or code to a form, and they automate everything.
  • The employee (end user) is everyone else. They meet the product only when it is in their way: a sign-in, a prompt, a blocked page. Success for them is not noticing it.
  • The buyer (CISO, Head of IT) cares about risk, audits and cost. They judge the product in a demo and a report, not in daily use.
  • Inside the company you will work with product managers, engineers, security researchers, solution engineers (who run demos and hear customers’ complaints first), support and technical writers. Solution engineers and support are your fastest route to real user problems.
  • One design often serves two people at once: a rule an admin writes becomes a message an employee sees. Always ask “who sees the result of this?”

Key terms

personaA short, research-based description of a type of user and what they need.
end userThe person who uses the product to do their real job, without managing it.
adminThe person who sets up and manages the product for others.
CISOChief Information Security Officer: the leader responsible for security, and often the buyer.
buyer versus userThe person who chooses and pays is often not the person who uses the product every day.
solution engineerA technical person on the sales team who runs demos and helps customers set up.
jobs to be doneWhat a person is trying to get done, in their words, regardless of the tool.

Skip or skim

Skip long persona templates and posters. A people map on one page is enough; you will refine it with real interviews later.

Check yourself

  • Who uses an access product every day, and who pays for it? Why does the difference matter for design?
  • Name two colleagues who could tell you about real user problems quickly.
  • What does success look like for an employee who meets a security product?

What a good answer looks like

  • The admin and the employee use it every day; the CISO or Head of IT usually decides and pays. The buyer judges it in demos and reports, the user lives with it, so you need to design for both: clear daily work and evidence that the risk is handled.
  • Solution engineers and support hear customer problems first: they see setup failures and “I can’t get in” tickets every week. Product managers and security researchers are also good sources.
  • They get to their work without noticing it. A good security product for employees is fast, quiet and, when it does block them, says why and what to do next.
