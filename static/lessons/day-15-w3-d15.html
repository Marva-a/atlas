Zero trust in plain words: check every request

Day 15 · Week 3 · Basics · about 120 min

Zero trust means no one is trusted just because they are “inside” the network: every request is checked for who is asking, from which device, and for what.

By the end, you can: Explain why zero trust checks identity, device and resource on every request, and describe the policy decision function and the policy enforcement point without treating a vendor's control/data plane as identical to NIST's model.

Why it matters to a designer

Every company in this course, Tailscale, Cloudflare, Tigera, sells a version of zero trust. If you can explain it in one plain sentence and know what it changes for admins and employees, you can talk to customers, engineers and interviewers in their language.

Key ideas

  • You already know (Week 2) that a classic VPN is one front door into the whole office network. Zero trust removes the idea of “inside is safe”.
  • Everyday picture: an old office checks your badge at the front door, then lets you into every room. A zero-trust office checks your badge at every door, and also checks you are wearing a visitor sticker if you are a visitor.
  • Three things are checked on every request: the identity (who), the device (is it a company laptop, is it up to date), and the resource (what are you trying to reach).
  • NIST splits the decision into a policy engine plus a policy administrator (together, the decision function) and a separate policy enforcement point, which lets traffic through or blocks it at the resource. In Tailscale, the coordination server plays the decision role and each device enforces — read that as resembling NIST’s model, not as an exact match to it.
  • ZTNA (zero-trust network access) gives access to one app or service, not the whole network. That limits the damage when one account is stolen.
  • Google’s BeyondCorp was an early real example: Google employees work from any network, and access depends on the user and device, not on being in the office.
  • For design, zero trust creates new moments: “Why am I blocked?”, “What do I need to fix on my device?”, and for admins, “Who can reach what right now?”

Key terms

zero trustA security approach where no request is trusted by default; each one is checked.
ZTNAZero-trust network access: access to specific apps or services, decided per request.
policy decision point (PDP)The part that decides whether a request is allowed.
policy enforcement point (PEP)The part that lets traffic through or blocks it, following the decision.
device postureFacts about a device, such as its operating system version or whether disk encryption is on.
BeyondCorpGoogle’s zero-trust model, where access depends on user and device, not network location.
perimeterThe old idea of a protected “inside” network with a wall around it.

Check yourself

  • Explain zero trust with the office-badge example.
  • What three things are checked on every request?
  • Why does ZTNA limit the damage of a stolen account more than a VPN?

What a good answer looks like

  • An old office checks your badge once at the front door and then trusts you everywhere. A zero-trust office checks your badge at every door, so being inside the building does not give you access to every room.
  • Who is asking (identity), from what (the device and its state) and for what (the resource or app). Some systems also look at context such as time or location.
  • A VPN usually puts you on the whole network, so a stolen account can reach many systems. ZTNA grants access only to the specific apps that account is allowed, so the attacker reaches far less.
