Exercise 9: MFA, passkeys and recovery

Day 25 · Week 4 · Intermediate · about 120 min

You will turn on one-time codes and passkeys in Keycloak, go through them as a user, and study the hardest part of sign-in: getting back in when you lose your device.

By the end, you can: Set up MFA and a passkey, and explain why passkeys resist phishing in ways one-time codes typically do not, without claiming every account-recovery path is equally phishing-resistant.

Why it matters to a designer

Sign-in and recovery are where security products meet every employee. Phishing-resistant methods like passkeys are spreading fast, and designers lead the work of making enrolment, fallback and recovery both safe and humane, including for people with disabilities.

Key ideas

  • From Day 2 you know the three factor types. Today you see them as screens: enrolment, daily use and recovery.
  • TOTP codes (the six digits in an authenticator app) are better than passwords alone, but people can still be tricked into typing them on a fake site.
  • Passkeys are phishing-resistant because the key only works on the real website it was made for: a fake site cannot trick it into signing in. That resistance covers the sign-in step itself — it does not make every related journey unphishable. Account recovery, a compromised device, session theft and social engineering around fallback methods are still real risks to design for.
  • Synced passkeys are copied across your devices through a cloud account (for example iCloud Keychain). Device-bound passkeys stay on one device or a security key. Synced is easier to recover; device-bound is stricter.
  • MFA fatigue (push bombing): attackers send many approval prompts until a tired person taps “approve”. Number matching and limits on prompts help.
  • Recovery is often the weakest point: if “forgot password” only needs an email, the strong MFA does not matter. Good recovery is safe and kind.
  • Accessible authentication (WCAG 2.2, 3.3.8): don’t force people to remember or transcribe things as the only way to sign in. Allow paste, password managers and passkeys.

Key terms

TOTPTime-based one-time password: the six-digit code from an authenticator app.
phishing-resistantA sign-in method that cannot be used on a fake site, such as passkeys or security keys.
synced passkeyA passkey copied across your devices by a cloud account.
device-bound passkeyA passkey that stays on one device or a hardware key.
MFA fatigueAttackers sending many approval prompts until someone accepts.
account recoveryHow someone gets back into their account after losing a password or device.
enrolmentSetting up a sign-in method for the first time.

Check yourself

  • Why are passkeys phishing-resistant?
  • What is MFA fatigue, and two ways to reduce it?
  • Why is account recovery often the weakest part of sign-in?

What a good answer looks like

  • The passkey is tied to the real website’s address. A fake site has a different address, so the passkey will not work there, and there is no code the person can be tricked into typing.
  • Attackers send many push prompts hoping a tired person approves one. Number matching (typing a number shown on screen) and limits or warnings on repeated prompts reduce it.
  • Because attackers can skip strong sign-in methods if recovery is weak, for example if a reset only needs access to an email account or a support call. Recovery must be as strong as sign-in, but still humane.
