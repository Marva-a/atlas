Exercise 12: break and fix a network policy

Day 32 · Week 5 · Part of the Network and Kubernetes Security pathway · Intermediate · about 120 min

You will turn on default deny, watch your app break, then write policies to fix it, noting exactly how you found the cause.

By the end, you can: Change a network policy, observe the resulting connectivity break, and explain why it happened.

Why it matters to a designer

This is the core pain of case study 2: default deny is safe but scary, because a missing rule silently breaks an app. Living through it once gives you the evidence and empathy to design a better way.

Key ideas

  • A default-deny policy selects every pod in a namespace and allows nothing. After applying it, your app will stop working. That is the point.
  • Breakage is often silent: requests time out, with no message that says “blocked by policy”. Finding the cause takes time and experience.
  • Fix it one connection at a time: web to orders, orders to the database, and DNS (pods must still reach the cluster’s DNS service, a common forgotten rule).
  • Test after each policy: does the app work, and is anything still allowed that should not be?
  • Write down how you found each cause. Your debugging path is research data for a tool that could make it faster.

Key terms

default denyA policy that blocks all traffic not explicitly allowed.
timeoutWhen a request waits for a reply that never comes.
allow ruleA policy rule that lets specific traffic through.
debuggingFinding and fixing the cause of a problem.

Check yourself

  • Why is default deny both safe and scary?
  • What rule is commonly forgotten after default deny, and why?
  • How did you find the cause of each break, and what would have made it faster?

What a good answer looks like

  • Safe because nothing can talk unless allowed, so an attacker cannot move freely. Scary because any missing rule breaks the app, often with no clear message.
  • Allowing DNS. Pods need to look up service names through the cluster’s DNS, so without that rule almost everything fails, even with the right app rules.
  • A good answer describes the real path (for example checking pods, guessing, reading logs, testing with a tool) and proposes something concrete, such as showing denied connections next to the policy that blocked them.
