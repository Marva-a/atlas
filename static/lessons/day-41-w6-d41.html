Triage view and tabletop: write it up and share

Day 41 · Week 6 · Advanced · about 120 min

You will write up the triage view and the tabletop as one story: how a team notices, decides and acts when something goes wrong.

By the end, you can: Document and present an alert-triage and incident-response design with its reasoning.

Why it matters to a designer

This piece shows you can design for experts under pressure, using real research. It covers questions hiring managers ask often: “How do you design for power users?” and “How do you handle complex data?”

Key ideas

  • Tell it as a story: the stolen-laptop incident, what the admin and analyst needed, and how your triage view helps.
  • Show evidence from your own exercises: times, missing context, slow screens.
  • Explain trade-offs openly, for example density versus clarity, or automatic grouping versus control.
  • Say what you would measure: time to triage, share of alerts dismissed as false, and missed real incidents.
  • Reflect: you are now working at an advanced level, judging real data and designing for experts. Note what still feels uncertain.

Key terms

trade-offA choice where gaining one thing means giving up some of another.
time to triageHow long it takes to decide what an alert needs.
power userAn expert user who uses a product heavily and wants speed and control.

Today's steps

Write the case (600–900 words) with the timeline, the triage view and its rationale.

55 min

Add a trade-offs section with at least two trade-offs and how you decided.

20 min

Ask a security engineer or a developer to review it, and note their comments.

20 min

Post a short version or save it in your portfolio folder.

10 min

Update your glossary and your people map: what did you learn about analysts?

15 min

Skip or skim

Do not explain every alert type. One incident and one view tell the story.

Check yourself

  • What trade-off in your triage view was hardest, and how did you decide?
  • How would you know your triage view is working?

What a good answer looks like

  • A good answer names both sides (for example showing more context versus keeping the list scannable) and the reason for the choice, ideally based on evidence from your exercises.
  • For example: shorter time to triage, fewer real incidents missed, and fewer alerts dismissed without a reason, measured before and after.
