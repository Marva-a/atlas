Access policies: groups, tags, ACLs and grants

Day 16 · Week 3 · Intermediate · about 120 min

An access policy is a set of rules that says which people and devices may reach which services; good policies are written for groups and roles, not for individuals.

By the end, you can: Write an access rule using source, destination and permission, explain the difference between ACLs and grants, and state that default-deny or default-allow behaviour depends on the specific policy engine's rules.

Why it matters to a designer

Writing access rules is the daily job of the admins you design for, and it is where the worst mistakes happen: a rule that is too open, or one that locks out the whole sales team. Case study 1 is about exactly this problem, so today you learn the vocabulary of rules.

Key ideas

  • From Week 1 you know roles and groups in Keycloak. Network access policies use the same ideas for devices and services.
  • A rule has a source (who), a destination (what), and what is allowed (for example a port, or “can use SSH”). Example: “group:design may reach tag:figma-proxy on port 443”.
  • Tags label machines by purpose instead of by owner. Example: tag:database or tag:prod. Rules about tags keep working when a new database is added.
  • In Tailscale, older rules are called ACLs and the newer form is grants. Both say who may reach what; grants can also carry application permissions.
  • Default deny: in Tailscale, ACLs are deny-by-default once you have a policy — if no rule allows something, it is blocked. But a policy file with no ACL section at all currently defaults to allowing everything, so “default deny” is a property of how a specific policy engine is configured, not a universal law every access-control product follows.
  • RBAC (role-based access control) gives access by role: “engineers may reach staging”. ABAC (attribute-based) uses facts: “engineers on a managed laptop during an incident”. ReBAC (relationship-based) uses links: “the owner of this document may share it”.
  • Where RBAC breaks: real companies need exceptions, so roles multiply (“engineer-staging-read-only-contractor”) until nobody understands them. This is called role explosion, and it is a design problem as much as a technical one.

Key terms

access policyA set of rules deciding who may reach what.
ACLAccess control list: rules that list who may access which resources.
grantIn Tailscale, the newer rule form that gives a source access to a destination, optionally with app permissions.
tagA label for a machine by purpose, such as tag:prod, used in rules.
default denyEverything is blocked unless a rule allows it.
RBACRole-based access control: access based on a person’s role.
ABACAttribute-based access control: access based on facts about the person, device or request.
ReBACRelationship-based access control: access based on relationships, such as owner or member.
role explosionWhen a company creates so many special roles that nobody can understand them.

What a good answer looks like

  • A source (who is asking: a user, group or tagged machine), a destination (what they want to reach) and what is allowed (ports, protocols or app permissions).
  • People and addresses change all the time. Groups and tags describe purpose, so when someone joins a team or a new server is added, the rule still works without editing it.
  • RBAC: “engineers may reach staging”. ABAC: “engineers may reach production only from a managed laptop”. ReBAC: “the owner of a document may share it with others”.
  • It is when special cases create hundreds of roles that nobody understands. It is a design problem because admins need ways to see, compare and clean up roles, not only to create them.
