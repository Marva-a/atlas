Least privilege, roles, reviews and audit

Day 43 · Week 7 · Advanced · about 120 min

Least privilege means each person and system has only the access it needs, for only as long as it needs it, and governance is how a company keeps it that way over time.

By the end, you can: Explain least privilege and just-in-time access, and state that 'explicit deny wins' is a rule of the specific policy engine in use, not a universal law.

Why it matters to a designer

Enterprise customers buy security products to prove control: who has admin rights, who approved them, when they were last checked. Designing roles, temporary access and access reviews is a senior-level problem that touches admins, managers, auditors and the buyer.

Key ideas

  • You met least privilege on Day 4 and policies in Week 3. Now you design the systems that keep access small over months and years.
  • Admin roles split power: not every IT person should be able to delete the whole tailnet. Example roles: owner, admin, network admin, IT admin, billing, auditor (read-only).
  • Just-in-time (JIT) access: instead of permanent admin rights, people request access for a reason and a time (two hours for an incident), it is approved, and it expires on its own.
  • Access reviews: every quarter, managers confirm who should keep which access. Badly designed reviews turn into “approve all”, which is called rubber-stamping.
  • In cloud IAM, an identity-based policy is attached to a person or role (“what can I do?”); a resource-based policy is attached to a resource (“who can use me?”). An explicit deny always wins over any allow.
  • Audit logs prove it all: who granted what, who approved it and when it ended. Auditors ask for this evidence.
  • Break-glass accounts are emergency admin accounts kept locked away for when normal sign-in fails. They must be rare, alarmed and reviewed.

Key terms

least privilegeOnly the access needed, only for as long as needed.
admin roleA named set of admin powers, such as network admin or auditor.
just-in-time accessTemporary access requested for a reason and removed automatically.
access reviewA regular check of who should keep which access.
rubber-stampingApproving everything in a review without really checking.
resource-based policyA policy attached to a resource that says who may use it.
explicit denyA rule that blocks access and overrides any allow, in the policy engines Atlas covers — this precedence is a product/engine design choice, not a universal rule of every authorization system.
break-glass accountAn emergency admin account used only when normal access fails.
separation of dutiesNo single person can complete a sensitive action alone.

Check yourself

  • What is just-in-time access, and why is it better than permanent admin rights?
  • Why do access reviews often fail, and what could design do about it?
  • How does an explicit deny interact with allows?

What a good answer looks like

  • Access is requested for a reason and a limited time, approved, and removed automatically. If an account is stolen, it usually has no standing admin rights to abuse, and every use is recorded.
  • Reviewers face long lists with little context, so they approve everything. Design can help with context (last used, risk level), smart defaults (suggest removing unused access) and smaller, focused reviews.
  • An explicit deny always wins. Even if another policy allows the action, the deny blocks it.
