Exercise 5: redesign a networking product onboarding

Day 12 · Week 2 · Basics · about 120 min

Using your Day 10 notes, you will redesign the first-run flow so that a new user reaches their first working connection faster and with more confidence.

By the end, you can: Redesign a flawed networking-product onboarding flow and justify the change against a usability principle.

Why it matters to a designer

Networking products are invisible when they work and confusing when they do not. The onboarding has to explain an invisible thing, guide two devices at once and handle failures clearly. This is a typical first design task for a new designer at a company like Tailscale.

Key ideas

  • Design for the “it works” moment: show clear progress (“laptop connected, now add a second device”) and celebrate the first connection.
  • Networking failures have causes users cannot see. A good error says what happened, what it means and one next step. Example: “Your phone has no internet connection. Connect to Wi‑Fi and try again.”
  • Onboarding spans devices: the user starts on a laptop and continues on a phone. Use a QR code or a link to make the jump easy.
  • Help in context usually beats a long tutorial: show the explanation at the moment it is needed.
  • Compare competitors: list how two other products handle the same first run, and borrow what works.

Key terms

onboardingThe first experience that takes a new user to their first success.
activationThe moment a new user gets real value; here, two devices connected.
empty stateWhat a screen shows before there is any data.
recoverable errorAn error that tells the user how to fix it and lets them continue.
contextual helpShort help shown in the place and moment it is needed.

Check yourself

  • What is the activation moment for a mesh VPN, and how does your design make it obvious?
  • Write one error message for a common connection failure, in plain words.

What a good answer looks like

  • Two of the user’s devices reaching each other. A good design shows progress towards it, confirms it clearly when it happens, and suggests the next useful step.
  • A good message says what happened, what it means and one action, without blame or jargon. For example: “Your laptop is signed out. Sign in again to reconnect.”
