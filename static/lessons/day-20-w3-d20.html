Case study 1 problem statement: write it up and share

Day 20 · Week 3 · Intermediate · about 120 min

You will write a clear problem statement for case study 1: who has the problem, what it is, the evidence, and what better would look like.

By the end, you can: Turn research findings into a defensible, evidence-backed problem statement.

Why it matters to a designer

A strong problem statement makes the rest of the case study easy to follow. It shows a hiring manager you understand the users and the business, and it gives you something to share with practitioners for early feedback.

Key ideas

  • A problem statement has four parts: the user (IT admin at a 50–500 person company), the problem (changing access rules is risky and hard to check), the evidence, and the impact (outages, over-exposed production, support load).
  • Write what better means as measurable outcomes: fewer access-related tickets, faster safe changes, admins able to answer “who can reach production?” in seconds.
  • Name your constraints: rules can be written in a file or a visual editor; many companies keep them in git; you cannot see real customer data.
  • Look at how bigger platforms package zero trust (Cloudflare One, Microsoft Global Secure Access) for context on what buyers expect.
  • Share a short version publicly or with one practitioner, and ask: “Does this match your experience?”

Key terms

problem statementA short description of the user, their problem, the evidence and why it matters.
outcomeThe change in the world you want, such as fewer mistakes, not a feature.
constraintA limit your design must respect.
success metricA number that shows whether the outcome is happening.

What a good answer looks like

  • The user, the problem, the evidence and the impact. Constraints and success metrics make it stronger.
  • For example: “An admin can answer ‘who can reach production?’ in under a minute” or “fewer support tickets after access changes”. A good metric is measurable and tied to the problem.
