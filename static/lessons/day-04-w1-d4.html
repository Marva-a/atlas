Accounts over time, what can go wrong, and how risks get ranked

Day 4 · Week 1 · Basics · about 210 min

People join, change teams and leave, and their access must change with them; threat modelling asks what could go wrong, and risk thinking decides what to fix first.

By the end, you can: Walk through a joiner-mover-leaver scenario and name where access was left over, threat-model a sign-in with the four questions and STRIDE, then write one risk as a full scenario, rate its likelihood and impact, and choose and justify a response.

Why it matters to a designer

Many real breaches start with access that should have been removed or a password that was stolen. Designers who understand the account lifecycle and basic threats design better invitations, offboarding flows, access reviews and warnings. And every security requirement you receive is a risk decision someone made: if you can read the scenario behind it, you can propose a design that reduces the same risk with less friction.

Key ideas

  • Joiner: someone new starts and needs the right access on day one. Example: Sam joins Sales and needs email, the CRM and the sales drive.
  • Mover: someone changes job inside the company. Example: Jordan moves from Sales to Finance. The risk: Jordan gets finance access but keeps sales access too. Over years, movers collect far more access than they need.
  • Leaver: someone leaves and all access must end, quickly. Example: a contractor’s project ends, but their account stays active for months: a stale account.
  • Least privilege means giving each person only the access they need, for as long as they need it. It is easy to say and hard to design for, which is why access reviews exist.
  • Phishing is tricking someone into giving away their password or approving a sign-in. Example: an email that looks like IT asking you to “confirm your account”. Stolen and phished credentials appear near the top of most industry incident reports, which is why phishing-resistant sign-in (Week 4) matters.
  • Threat modelling asks four questions: What are we working on? What can go wrong? What are we going to do about it? Did we do a good job? STRIDE is a checklist for the second question: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service and elevation of privilege.
  • Threat, vulnerability, risk: a threat is something bad that could happen (a thief), a vulnerability is a weakness (an unlocked window), and risk is how likely and how harmful it is together. The rest of this lesson turns that into decisions.
  • Risk is a scenario, not a single word: a threat exploits a vulnerability to harm an asset, with some likelihood and some impact. Example: an attacker (threat) uses a leaked password (vulnerability) to read customer records (asset), which is fairly likely and very harmful.
  • Likelihood is how plausible the scenario is, given who might try and how hard it is. Impact is how much harm it would do: money, safety, trust, legal duties. Both depend on context: the same weakness is minor on a test server and serious in production.
  • Teams rank risks, often on a simple grid of likelihood by impact, because time and money are limited. A rough, honest ranking beats a precise-looking number nobody can defend.
  • There are four responses. Reduce (mitigate): add controls. Accept: knowingly live with it. Transfer: share it, for example through insurance or a supplier contract. Avoid: stop doing the risky thing. Accepting a risk is a valid decision when it is explicit and owned.
  • Residual risk is what is left after controls. No control makes risk zero, so good teams say what remains and who owns it.

Key terms

joiner, mover, leaverThe three moments of an account’s life: starting, changing jobs and leaving.
stale accountAn account no longer needed or used, but still active.
least privilegeGiving only the access someone needs, only for as long as they need it.
access reviewA regular check where a manager confirms who should keep which access.
phishingTricking someone into giving away their sign-in or approving a fake request.
threat modelA structured look at what could go wrong with a system and what to do about it.
STRIDEA checklist of six kinds of threat: spoofing, tampering, repudiation, information disclosure, denial of service, elevation of privilege.
vulnerabilityA weakness that a threat could use.
threatSomething that could cause harm: an attacker, an insider, a mistake or a natural event.
riskA scenario where a threat exploits a vulnerability to harm an asset, judged by likelihood and impact.
likelihoodHow plausible a harmful scenario is in a given context.
impactHow much harm a scenario would cause if it happened.
mitigateReduce a risk by adding controls.
risk acceptanceKnowingly deciding to live with a risk, with a named owner.
residual riskThe risk that remains after controls are in place.

Skip or skim

Skip the long threat-modelling methods (attack trees, PASTA), quantitative risk methods (annualised loss, Monte Carlo models) and certification-style risk frameworks. Designers need to ask what could go wrong and question risk decisions, not run formal assessments.

Check yourself

  • Why are movers a risk?
  • What are the four questions of threat modelling?
  • Threat, vulnerability and risk: explain the difference with a house example.
  • Rewrite 'phishing is a risk' as a proper risk scenario.
  • What are the four ways to respond to a risk? Give an example of each.
  • Why can a team reasonably accept a risk instead of fixing it?

What a good answer looks like

  • When people change jobs they often get new access without losing the old access. Over time they can reach much more than they need, so a stolen account or a mistake does more damage.
  • What are we working on? What can go wrong? What are we going to do about it? Did we do a good job?
  • A thief is the threat, an unlocked window is the vulnerability, and the risk is how likely it is that a thief uses that window and how much they could take.
  • For example: an attacker (threat) sends a convincing fake sign-in page to staff, and some staff can be tricked into entering their password and one-time code (vulnerability), letting the attacker read company email (asset); plausible for any company with email, with high impact if an executive is affected.
  • Reduce: require phishing-resistant passkeys. Accept: allow an old internal tool to keep password sign-in until it is retired next quarter, with an owner. Transfer: buy cyber insurance or make a supplier contractually responsible. Avoid: stop storing card numbers at all and use a payment provider.
  • Because fixing it might cost more than the harm it prevents, or the likelihood is very low, or a bigger risk needs the effort first. Acceptance is fine when it is a deliberate, recorded decision with someone accountable, not an oversight.
