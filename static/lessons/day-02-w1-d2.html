Who you are and what you may do: sign-in basics

Day 2 · Week 1 · Basics · about 120 min

Authentication proves who you are, authorization decides what you may do, and accounting records what happened.

By the end, you can: Distinguish authentication, authorization and accounting, and identify the factor type (knowledge, possession or inherence) behind a given sign-in method.

Why it matters to a designer

Sign-in is the part of security every user touches. Designers in this field design sign-in, recovery, MFA prompts and “you don’t have access” pages, and each one is a balance between safety and getting people to their work.

Key ideas

  • Authentication (AuthN) answers “who are you?”. Example: typing your password and approving a prompt on your phone.
  • Authorization (AuthZ) answers “what may you do?”. Example: you can view the shared budget sheet but not edit it.
  • Accounting answers “what happened?” by recording activity, like the sheet’s history showing who changed cell B4 and when. A security audit log is related but broader: it can record security-relevant events, such as sign-ins or permission changes, without being the same thing as AAA’s resource-usage accounting.
  • There are three kinds of proof, called factors: something you know (a password), something you have (your phone, a security key) and something you are (a fingerprint). MFA means using two or more kinds.
  • A credential is your proof (a password, a passkey). A session is the time you stay signed in after proving it. A token is the digital ticket an app carries to show you already signed in, like a cinema wristband that lets you back in without buying a new ticket.
  • Passkeys replace passwords with a key stored on your device and unlocked with your face, fingerprint or PIN. They cannot be phished the way passwords can. You will design for them in Week 4.
  • Design tension: every extra check adds safety and friction. Good products ask for more proof only when the risk is higher, for example a new device or a sensitive action.

Check yourself

  • Explain authentication, authorization and accounting with one example from your own life.
  • What is the difference between a credential, a session and a token?
  • Why do good products not ask for MFA on every single action?

What a good answer looks like

  • For example, at work: you sign in with a password and a phone prompt (authentication), you can open the design drive but not payroll (authorization), and the drive records who opened which file (accounting).
  • The credential is the proof you give (a password or passkey). The session is how long you stay signed in afterwards. The token is the ticket the app carries during that session to show you already proved who you are.
  • Each check adds friction, and people find ways around rules that slow them down. Asking for more proof only when risk is higher (a new device, a payment, an admin change) keeps both safety and speed.
