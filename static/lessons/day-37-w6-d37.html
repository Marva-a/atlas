Detection and incident response in plain words

Day 37 · Week 6 · Intermediate · about 120 min

Detection is spotting an attack in your data, and incident response is the organised way a team handles it, from first alert to lessons learned.

By the end, you can: Explain what MITRE ATT&CK is (a knowledge base of techniques, not an incident-response process) and outline the stages of incident response separately.

Why it matters to a designer

Incidents are where security products prove their worth. Designers who know how analysts think, and how incidents unfold, can design investigation views, timelines and handoffs that save time when it matters most.

Key ideas

  • MITRE ATT&CK is a shared catalogue of what attackers do, grouped by goal: getting in, staying in, stealing credentials, moving around, taking data. Teams use it as a common language.
  • Credential access (TA0006) is the group of techniques for stealing passwords and tokens, for example phishing or dumping saved passwords. Identity products defend here.
  • The incident-response lifecycle (NIST SP 800-61): prepare, detect and analyse, contain, eradicate and recover, then learn lessons. The newer revision links these to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework.
  • Roles in an incident: an incident commander who coordinates, responders who investigate, and someone who communicates with leaders and customers.
  • From Day 11: many incidents start with broken access control, such as BOLA (changing an id to reach someone else's data). Its cousin BFLA, broken function level authorization, is a normal user calling an admin-only action. Both show up in detection as odd access patterns, not failed sign-ins.

Key terms

detectionFinding signs of an attack in data.
MITRE ATT&CKA public catalogue of attacker tactics and techniques.
tacticAn attacker’s goal, such as credential access.
techniqueA way of achieving a tactic, such as phishing.
incidentA security event that needs an organised response.
containmentStopping an attack from spreading, for example disabling an account.
postmortemA blameless review after an incident, to learn and improve.
BFLABroken function level authorization: doing actions you should not be allowed to do.

What a good answer looks like

  • It gives teams a shared language for attacker behaviour, grouped by goal (tactics) and method (techniques), so they can plan detections and describe incidents consistently.
  • Preparation; detection and analysis; containment, eradication and recovery; and post-incident activity (lessons learned). Rev. 3 frames them around the CSF functions.
  • Broken object level authorization: an app checks that you are signed in but not that the object is yours. Changing /orders/123 to /orders/124 shows someone else’s order.
