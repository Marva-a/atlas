Exercise 17: a security UX review

Day 52 · Week 8 · Advanced · about 120 min

You will review one real security product against the patterns that keep people safe: warnings, safe defaults and confirming dangerous actions.

By the end, you can: Review a security experience for safe defaults, clear warnings and a recoverable path, and identify one change that reduces risk without adding friction.

Why it matters to a designer

Security UX patterns appear in every product in this domain. A sharp, fair review shows you can spot risk in an interface and propose precise fixes, a skill interviewers test with “critique this screen”.

Key ideas

  • Safe defaults: the default setting should be the secure one. Example: new tailnets deny nothing by default in some products and everything in others; which is right for whom?
  • Warnings must be rare and specific. A warning on every action is ignored, like alert fatigue for users.
  • Confirming dangerous actions: make the consequence clear (“This removes access for 240 people”), name the object, and for the worst actions, ask people to type its name. Offer undo where possible.
  • Errors should say what happened, why, and what to do next, without blame or jargon.
  • CISA’s Secure by Design (Day 6) asks vendors to make products secure out of the box, so customers do not have to configure safety themselves.
  • Review fairly: what works, the three most important problems with evidence, and specific fixes.

Key terms

safe defaultA default setting that is the secure choice.
secure by designBuilding security into a product from the start rather than adding it later.
destructive actionAn action that removes or breaks something, often hard to undo.
confirmation dialogA prompt asking the user to confirm an action.
undoReversing a recent action.

Skip or skim

Do not review the whole product. Pick the screens where risk is highest.

Check yourself

  • What makes a confirmation for a dangerous action effective?
  • Why should warnings be rare?

What a good answer looks like

  • It states the consequence in specific terms, names exactly what will be affected, uses a clear action label instead of “OK”, and for the worst actions asks for extra effort such as typing the name. Undo is better still when possible.
  • If warnings appear all the time, people learn to dismiss them without reading, so they miss the one that matters.
